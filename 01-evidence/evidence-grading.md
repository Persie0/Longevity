# Evidence overview: diet, body composition and longevity

## Bottom line

The most defensible dietary pattern for long-term health is a **Mediterranean-style, minimally processed, plant-forward diet** rather than a narrow branded diet or a collection of “superfoods”. The core pattern is supported by randomized cardiovascular-outcome data plus large prospective and meta-analytic evidence.

The main recurring components are:

- vegetables and whole fruit every day;
- legumes/soy most days;
- whole grains instead of refined grains;
- nuts and seeds daily or near-daily;
- extra-virgin olive oil and other unsaturated fats replacing butter/lard/coconut-heavy patterns;
- fish/seafood regularly, especially oily low-mercury species;
- optional plain fermented dairy such as yogurt/kefir;
- adequate protein for age/activity;
- modest eggs and unprocessed poultry if desired;
- low red-meat intake and near-zero processed meat;
- minimal sugar-sweetened beverages and industrial trans fat;
- little or no alcohol;
- energy intake matched to body-composition and training goals rather than chronic overfeeding.

No single food, fasting schedule, supplement or meal-timing strategy has evidence strong enough to compensate for a poor overall pattern.

## 1. What evidence is strongest?

### Mediterranean dietary pattern

PREDIMED is unusually important because most nutrition/longevity evidence is observational. In the corrected/reanalyzed trial, 7,447 adults at high cardiovascular risk were randomized to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with nuts, or a reduced-fat control diet. The primary cardiovascular composite was lower in both Mediterranean groups: approximately HR 0.69 for the extra-virgin-olive-oil group and HR 0.72 for the nuts group versus control.

Important caveat: protocol deviations required reanalysis, and the population was at elevated cardiovascular risk rather than young healthy adults. The trial nevertheless provides stronger causal evidence for the overall dietary pattern than exists for almost any individual “longevity food”.

Source: https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1800389

### Fibre and carbohydrate quality

The Lancet 2019 series of systematic reviews/meta-analyses found consistently lower rates of coronary disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer with higher fibre/whole-grain intake. Benefits were most evident when fibre intake reached roughly **25–29 g/day**, with dose-response data suggesting higher intakes could confer additional benefit.

A practical target of **30–40 g/day** is therefore reasonable for many adults if tolerated, while ~25–30 g/day is a useful minimum range. Increase gradually, especially if currently eating little fibre.

Source: Reynolds et al. 2019, PMID 30638909, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9.

### Whole grains

A 2016 dose-response meta-analysis of 45 prospective studies found each 90 g/day increase in whole grains was associated with lower coronary heart disease (RR 0.81), cardiovascular disease (RR 0.78), and all-cause mortality (RR 0.83). These are observational associations and should not be read as exact causal effects for an individual, but the consistency, dose-response and replacement logic strongly support whole grains over refined grains.

Source: https://www.bmj.com/content/353/bmj.i2716

### Fruit and vegetables

A 2021 analysis combining the Nurses’ Health Study, Health Professionals Follow-Up Study and 24 additional cohorts found the lowest mortality around **5 servings/day**, approximately **2 fruit + 3 vegetable servings**. Relative to 2 servings/day, 5 servings/day was associated with HR 0.87 for all-cause mortality. The association plateaued around five servings in that analysis. Fruit juice and potatoes did not show the same pattern.

Again, this is prospective observational evidence, not randomized proof that exactly five servings is a biological optimum.

Source: Wang et al. Circulation 2021, PMID 33641343, DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996.

### Legumes

A 2023 dose-response meta-analysis of prospective studies included over 1.1 million participants and found higher legume intake associated with modestly lower all-cause mortality (highest vs lowest HR about 0.94). Certainty was low-to-moderate, but legumes are additionally supported by their fibre, potassium, magnesium, plant protein and displacement of processed/red meat.

Source: PMID 36811595, DOI 10.1016/j.advnut.2022.10.009.

### Nuts

Prospective evidence consistently associates nut intake with lower cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. The practical benefit is strongest when nuts replace refined snacks, butter-rich foods or processed meat rather than simply adding calories to an already excessive diet.

Practical dose: roughly **20–30 g/day**.

### Extra-virgin olive oil and unsaturated fats

The key principle is substitution. Replacing saturated/trans fats with unsaturated fats is much better supported than the simplistic idea that “all fat” should be minimized.

Extra-virgin olive oil is the best-supported default added fat within the Mediterranean evidence base. Canola/rapeseed and other unsaturated plant oils are also reasonable.

### Fish and seafood

Fish combines complete protein with EPA/DHA, selenium and other micronutrients. AHA guidance commonly recommends **2 fish servings/week**, particularly fatty fish. Prefer salmon, sardines, herring, trout and Atlantic/Pacific chub mackerel for a favorable omega-3-to-mercury profile.

Tuna is useful but less ideal as the default fish because mercury varies by species. Canned light/skipjack is lower in mercury; albacore/white and yellowfin are higher; bigeye is a high-mercury species.

Sources:
- https://www.heart.org/en/healthy-living/healthy-eating/eat-smart/fats/fish-and-omega-3-fatty-acids
- https://www.fda.gov/food/consumers/advice-about-eating-fish

### Processed and red meat

Processed meat shows the most consistent adverse association with mortality/CVD/cancer outcomes and is the clearest meat category to minimize. Red meat associations are weaker and more heterogeneous, especially for unprocessed lean meat.

Practical default:
- processed meat: **rare/near-zero**;
- unprocessed red meat: **0–2 servings/week** if desired;
- use legumes/soy/fish as recurring replacements.

## 2. Dietary fats: what matters most

### Saturated fat is primarily a replacement question

Reducing saturated fat is most useful when it is replaced with polyunsaturated/monounsaturated fat or high-quality carbohydrate such as whole grains and legumes. Replacing butter with refined sugar/flour is not the goal.

Prioritize:
- extra-virgin olive oil;
- nuts/seeds;
- avocado;
- fish;
- canola/rapeseed oil.

Limit as dominant fats:
- butter/ghee;
- lard;
- coconut/palm-heavy patterns;
- processed meat fats;
- pastries/fried packaged foods.

Industrial trans fat should be kept as close to zero as practical.

## 3. Sodium, potassium and blood pressure

WHO recommends sodium below **2 g/day**, equivalent to about **5 g salt/day**, for the general population. This is a population target, not a requirement to weigh every crystal of salt.

High-potassium foods—beans, lentils, potatoes, fruit, vegetables, yogurt—support a favorable dietary pattern. People with kidney disease or medications affecting potassium need individualized advice.

Highly active endurance athletes can require much more sodium around prolonged sweating; sports replacement is a separate context from chronic high-sodium eating.

Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet

## 4. Free sugars and refined carbohydrate

WHO recommends free sugars below **10% of energy**, with lower intakes offering additional dental/metabolic advantages.

Highest-value reductions:
- sugar-sweetened beverages;
- large juice intakes;
- sweets/pastries as daily foods;
- refined snack foods that displace nutrient-dense foods.

Carbohydrate itself is not the problem. Whole grains, potatoes, legumes and fruit are compatible with excellent cardiometabolic health and support training performance.

## 5. Alcohol

Alcohol should **not** be started or maintained for presumed cardiovascular protection. WHO Europe states that no level of alcohol consumption is risk-free for health, largely because cancer risk begins at low exposure.

For a longevity-focused pattern, **zero is a valid default** and less is generally safer.

Source: https://www.who.int/europe/news-room/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health

## 6. Dairy and fermented foods

Total dairy appears broadly neutral in large cohort/meta-analyses; fermented dairy such as yogurt may show modest favorable associations. These data do not establish that dairy is required.

Useful choices:
- plain yogurt/kefir;
- skyr/Greek yogurt/quark for protein;
- modest cheese if desired.

Fermented vegetables (kimchi, sauerkraut) and tempeh/miso can add variety. Microbiome effects are biologically interesting, but no fermented-food protocol has demonstrated lifespan extension.

## 7. Protein and muscle preservation

For active adults, particularly with resistance training:
- **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** is a strong general range;
- **~1.6 g/kg/day** is a useful hypertrophy target;
- much more than ~1.6 g/kg/day usually produces little additional lean-mass benefit when training and energy are adequate.

Protein quality and meal distribution matter less than meeting total daily needs, but spreading protein over 3–4 meals is practical.

See `03-muscle/protein-and-hypertrophy.md`.

## 8. Energy balance and body composition

### Longevity-oriented default

Avoid chronic energy excess that drives progressive central adiposity and metabolic dysfunction. At the same time, aggressive chronic calorie restriction can impair training, bone health, hormones and lean mass.

### Muscle gain

A small surplus—roughly **5–10% above maintenance**—is usually enough for modest gain. Faster weight gain does not mean proportionally faster muscle gain and often adds unnecessary fat.

Track:
- body-weight trend over weeks, not days;
- waist circumference;
- gym performance;
- recovery/satiety.

## 9. Meal frequency, fasting and time-restricted eating

Intermittent fasting/time-restricted eating can help some people control calorie intake because it simplifies eating. However:
- there is no convincing evidence that a particular fasting window extends human lifespan independent of calorie intake and diet quality;
- narrow windows can make adequate protein distribution harder for muscle-oriented people;
- late large meals may worsen sleep/reflux in some people.

Use timing as an adherence tool rather than a “longevity hack”.

## 10. Ultra-processed foods

“Ultra-processed” is a broad category. The most useful practical focus is not a purity rule but displacement and formulation:
- prioritize foods with intact structure, fibre and protein;
- reduce products engineered around refined starch, sugar, fat and salt that are easy to overeat;
- do not fear useful processed foods such as frozen vegetables, canned beans, plain yogurt, tofu, whole-grain bread or protein powder simply because processing occurred.

## 11. Practical daily plate

At most meals:
- **1/2 plate:** non-starchy vegetables;
- **1/4 plate:** protein source;
- **1/4 plate:** whole grain, potatoes or other minimally processed carbohydrate;
- extra-virgin olive oil, nuts/seeds, herbs/spices as appropriate;
- whole fruit as the default dessert/snack.

For muscle gain, increase the protein and/or carbohydrate portions to meet training and energy needs.

## 12. What is not supported as a major longevity lever

Evidence does **not** justify making the following central strategies:
- avoiding all seed oils;
- ketogenic diets for everyone;
- carnivore diets;
- raw-food-only diets;
- juice cleanses/detoxes;
- alkaline diets;
- chronically very-low-fat diets;
- glucose-spike avoidance at the expense of overall diet quality;
- arbitrary “anti-nutrient” avoidance;
- expensive superfood powders;
- fasting for “autophagy” as if human lifespan extension were proven;
- routine megadose vitamins/antioxidants.

The basics—diet quality, body composition, physical activity, sleep, smoking avoidance and management of major clinical risk factors—dominate the evidence.

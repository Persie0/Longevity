# Nutrition deep dive: evidence, dose-response, trade-offs and practical decisions

This file expands the shorter food-group notes. The emphasis is on what changes outcomes, not on ranking foods by isolated nutrient density.

## 1. Dietary pattern matters more than individual superfoods

The strongest intervention evidence supports a Mediterranean-style pattern rich in extra-virgin olive oil, nuts, vegetables, legumes, whole grains and fish. PREDIMED is especially important because it tested dietary patterns against clinical cardiovascular outcomes rather than only biomarkers.

The practical lesson is not that olive oil or nuts are magical. The benefit comes from the **whole substitution pattern**: unsaturated fats instead of butter/processed meat, intact plants instead of refined foods, and fish/legumes replacing less favorable protein choices.

## 2. Vegetables and fruit

### Dose-response

Large prospective meta-analyses consistently show lower mortality and cardiovascular risk with higher fruit/vegetable intake. A useful practical target is around **5 servings/day**, often approximated as **2 fruit + 3 vegetables**, while some datasets show continued risk reduction at higher intakes for selected outcomes.

### Best recurring categories

No vegetable is proven to be uniquely superior. Diversity across these groups is more defensible:

- leafy greens: spinach, kale, rocket, bok choy;
- crucifers: broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts;
- alliums: onions, garlic, leeks;
- red/orange: tomatoes, peppers, carrots, pumpkin;
- legumes (botanically separate but functionally important): lentils, beans, peas;
- mushrooms;
- berries/citrus/apples/pears/kiwi and other whole fruits.

### Raw vs cooked

A mix is ideal. Cooking improves availability of some carotenoids/lycopene and reduces volume, making vegetables easier to eat. Raw preparation preserves some heat-sensitive vitamins. Steaming, microwaving, roasting and sautéing are all reasonable. Avoid turning vegetables into a vehicle for large amounts of deep-frying/saturated fat.

### Whole fruit vs juice

Whole fruit is more satiating and preserves structure/fibre. Juice is easier to overconsume and is less favorable for glycaemic control. Smoothies are intermediate: nutritionally useful but easy to drink quickly.

## 3. Whole grains and fibre

### Fibre

High fibre intake is associated with lower cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer and all-cause mortality. Randomized data also support LDL and glycaemic benefits for specific viscous fibres.

A practical target:

- **minimum floor:** ~25–30 g/day;
- **strong practical range:** ~30–40 g/day if tolerated.

Increase gradually to avoid bloating and ensure fluid intake.

### Whole grains

Whole-grain intake has a consistent dose-response association with lower cardiovascular and mortality risk. Useful staples:

- oats;
- barley;
- rye;
- whole-wheat bread/pasta;
- brown rice;
- bulgur;
- buckwheat;
- quinoa.

The exact grain matters much less than replacing refined grains and maintaining overall diet quality.

## 4. Legumes and soy

Legumes combine protein, fermentable fibre, potassium, magnesium and low-glycaemic carbohydrate.

A strong default is **one serving most days**, roughly **150–250 g cooked** in a main meal.

### Soy

Soy protein quality is high among plant foods. Tofu, tempeh, edamame and soy milk are practical muscle-oriented choices. Human evidence does not support the common claim that normal soy intake feminizes men or meaningfully lowers testosterone.

## 5. Nuts and seeds

Nuts are energy-dense but repeatedly associated with favorable cardiovascular and mortality outcomes.

Practical target: **~20–30 g/day**.

High-value rotation:

- walnuts (ALA omega-3);
- almonds;
- pistachios;
- hazelnuts;
- peanuts;
- pumpkin/sesame seeds;
- ground flax and chia.

Ground flax/chia add ALA and fibre; **1–2 tbsp/day** is a practical amount.

## 6. Fats: quality and substitution

The health effect of saturated fat depends heavily on what replaces it.

Best-supported replacements:

- extra-virgin olive oil;
- canola/rapeseed oil;
- nuts/seeds;
- avocado;
- fish;
- high-fibre carbohydrate.

Less favorable pattern:

- butter/ghee as main fat;
- fatty processed meat;
- coconut/palm fat as dominant fat;
- pastries/ultra-processed snacks where saturated fat accompanies refined flour/sugar.

Extra-virgin olive oil has particularly strong Mediterranean-diet outcome data, but it should not become unlimited calories.

## 7. Fish and seafood

### Why fish is useful

Fish provides high-quality protein and, in oily species, EPA/DHA. Food-based fish intake has stronger health evidence than universal fish-oil supplementation.

### Best recurring oily fish

- salmon;
- sardines;
- herring;
- trout;
- Atlantic/Pacific chub mackerel (not king mackerel).

### Tuna hierarchy

- canned light/skipjack: generally lower mercury; best routine tuna choice;
- albacore/white: higher mercury, eat less often;
- yellowfin: also higher;
- bigeye: high mercury and poor routine choice.

### Frequency

**2–3 seafood meals/week**, with 1–2 oily low-mercury meals, is a strong default.

## 8. Eggs, poultry, red meat and processed meat

### Eggs

Eggs are nutrient-dense and practical. For most healthy adults, **0–1 egg/day on average** fits well in a high-quality diet. Individual LDL response varies; someone with very high LDL/ApoB may need more personalized dietary management.

### Poultry

Unprocessed chicken/turkey is a reasonable lean protein but not uniquely protective. Use it as one option among fish, legumes, soy and dairy.

### Red meat

A conservative longevity-oriented pattern uses **0–2 servings/week**, emphasizing unprocessed lean cuts.

### Processed meat

Bacon, salami, sausages, hot dogs and cured deli meats have the clearest adverse observational evidence and should be **rare or near-zero**.

## 9. Dairy and fermented foods

Total dairy is broadly neutral in many prospective datasets, while yogurt/fermented dairy sometimes shows modestly favorable associations.

Useful choices:

- plain Greek yogurt;
- skyr;
- kefir;
- cottage cheese/quark;
- plain yogurt.

These are especially helpful when a higher protein intake is desired.

Cheese can fit in moderate portions but contributes sodium and saturated fat. Butter is better treated as flavoring than a primary fat.

Fermented foods may influence microbiome/immune markers, but no fermented food has proven lifespan-extending effects in humans.

## 10. Protein intake for muscle and healthy aging

### Active adults

- general active maintenance: **~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**;
- modest hypertrophy: **~1.6 g/kg/day** is a strong evidence-based target.

The classic Morton meta-analysis found diminishing hypertrophy returns above roughly **1.6 g/kg/day** when resistance training and energy intake were adequate.

### Per meal

A practical target is **~0.3–0.4 g/kg per meal**, over 3–4 meals.

For a 75-kg person:

- 0.3 g/kg ≈ 23 g;
- 0.4 g/kg ≈ 30 g.

Total daily protein is more important than perfect distribution.

### Plant protein

Plant-based hypertrophy is entirely feasible. Soy is especially convenient; pea+rice blends improve amino-acid balance. A slightly higher total intake can compensate for lower digestibility/leucine content.

## 11. Energy balance and body composition

For health, avoid chronic overfeeding. For modest muscle gain, a **small surplus (~5–10%)** is enough for many people.

Track:

- body-weight trend;
- waist circumference;
- gym performance;
- subjective recovery.

If weight/waist increases rapidly while strength changes slowly, the surplus is too large.

## 12. Carbohydrates and training

Carbohydrate is not inherently harmful. High-quality carbohydrate supports training and can coexist with excellent metabolic health.

Strong choices:

- oats;
- barley;
- potatoes;
- rice;
- whole-grain bread/pasta;
- fruit;
- legumes.

Very-low-carbohydrate diets are not required for longevity and may make endurance/high-volume resistance training harder.

## 13. Sodium

WHO recommends **<2 g sodium/day**, equivalent to about **5 g salt/day**, for the general population.

High sodium raises blood pressure, though sensitivity varies. People who sweat heavily during long endurance exercise may need more sodium around training without needing a high-sodium baseline diet.

## 14. Free sugar and ultra-processed foods

WHO recommends free sugars <10% of energy, ideally lower. Sugar-sweetened beverages are the clearest target because they add energy with little satiety.

“Ultra-processed” is a useful population-level concept but should not become a simplistic rule. Some processed foods—fortified whole-grain bread, canned beans, tofu, frozen vegetables, yogurt—remain highly compatible with a healthy diet.

## 15. Alcohol

Alcohol should not be started for health. Cancer risk rises with intake and there is no reliable “protective dose” recommendation for longevity. Zero is a valid default; less is better.

## 16. Fasting and time-restricted eating

Intermittent fasting can help some people control calorie intake and simplify eating. However, evidence does not show that a specific fasting window extends human lifespan independent of calories and diet quality.

For muscle gain, avoid fasting schedules that make it hard to reach protein/energy targets.

## 17. Food diversity and the microbiome

Higher plant diversity is a reasonable practical goal, but popular thresholds such as “30 plants/week” are not biological cutoffs proven in clinical trials.

A useful heuristic is **20–30 different plant foods/week**, counting vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices—mainly because diversity makes nutrient/fibre variety easier.

## 18. What matters most in practice

Priority order:

1. total diet quality and energy balance;
2. enough vegetables/fruit/fibre;
3. legumes/whole grains/nuts/seeds;
4. unsaturated fats replacing saturated/processed-fat sources;
5. adequate protein and resistance training;
6. fish/seafood or targeted omega-3 strategy;
7. low processed meat, sugary drinks and alcohol;
8. micronutrient gaps addressed specifically;
9. supplements only after the basics are solved.

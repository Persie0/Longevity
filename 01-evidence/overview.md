# Evidence grading and how to read this repository

Nutrition, exercise and supplement claims are unusually easy to overstate. This file defines the standards used throughout the repository so that a mechanistic hypothesis, a biomarker change and a reduction in real disease events are not treated as equivalent evidence.

## 1. Outcome hierarchy

When judging whether an intervention is worth doing, outcomes are weighted approximately in this order:

### Highest-value outcomes: hard clinical outcomes

Examples:
- all-cause mortality;
- cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction or stroke;
- cancer incidence/mortality;
- fractures;
- dementia diagnosis;
- hospitalization;
- disability, falls and loss of physical function;
- clinically important symptoms and quality of life.

A supplement that changes a laboratory marker but has never shown benefit on a meaningful health outcome should not be called a proven “longevity” intervention.

### Intermediate outcomes

Examples:
- LDL cholesterol / ApoB;
- blood pressure;
- HbA1c and fasting glucose;
- VO2max;
- measured strength and lean mass;
- bone mineral density;
- validated cognitive test scores;
- exercise time-trial performance.

These can be highly useful. For example, LDL/ApoB and blood pressure are established causal cardiovascular risk factors, and VO2max/strength are meaningful functional outcomes. But a change in an intermediate endpoint still requires context: magnitude, baseline risk, durability, adverse effects and whether better-proven alternatives exist.

### Low-confidence surrogate/mechanistic outcomes

Examples:
- NAD+ concentration;
- one inflammatory cytokine;
- antioxidant capacity;
- mitochondrial gene-expression markers;
- “autophagy markers”;
- acute testosterone or growth-hormone changes;
- microbiome diversity without a clinical endpoint;
- epigenetic clocks without demonstrated downstream health benefit.

These outcomes can generate hypotheses. They do **not** establish longer lifespan, disease prevention or clinically meaningful rejuvenation by themselves.

## 2. Study-design hierarchy

### Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)

Best for causal questions when they are adequately powered, long enough, well blinded where possible, and analyze clinically relevant endpoints. Limitations still matter: short duration, selective populations, industry funding, attrition, multiple outcomes and surrogate endpoints can all distort interpretation.

### Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs

Useful when trials are sufficiently similar. A meta-analysis does not automatically create high-quality evidence; pooling many small, biased or heterogeneous trials can produce a precise-looking but unreliable estimate.

Questions asked:
- Were trials randomized and appropriately controlled?
- Was heterogeneity high?
- Were outcomes prespecified?
- Was publication bias likely?
- Is the effect clinically meaningful, not merely statistically significant?
- Does the estimate survive sensitivity analyses?

### Prospective cohort studies

Very important for long-term diet, physical activity and mortality questions where multi-decade randomized trials are often impossible. However, residual confounding is unavoidable. Health-conscious people who eat more whole grains, nuts or vegetables may also smoke less, exercise more, have better healthcare access and differ in many unmeasured ways.

Observational dose-response evidence is therefore used to design a sensible pattern, but is not described as proof that a specific food causes a precise percentage reduction in mortality.

### Mechanistic, animal and cell studies

Useful for biological plausibility and generating research questions. They are not sufficient to recommend a supplement for human longevity.

A compound extending lifespan in worms or mice is especially weak evidence for extending lifespan in healthy humans unless replicated in human outcome trials.

## 3. Repository A–E decision grade

The grade is a **practical decision grade**, not a formal GRADE rating.

### A — Strong evidence / worthwhile for the relevant goal

Characteristics:
- repeated evidence from good RCTs/meta-analyses or authoritative consensus;
- meaningful and reproducible outcome;
- known practical dose/implementation;
- acceptable safety profile when correctly used.

Examples:
- resistance training for strength/hypertrophy;
- creatine monohydrate for improving training-related strength/lean-mass outcomes;
- adequate dietary protein for muscle gain;
- caffeine for acute performance;
- psyllium for lowering LDL when used at effective doses.

An A grade does **not** mean everyone must use the intervention. Caffeine can be Grade A for exercise performance and still be a bad choice for someone whose sleep deteriorates.

### B — Useful in specific situations

Characteristics:
- strong evidence in a subgroup, deficiency, life stage, clinical context or narrowly defined sport demand;
- not useful enough to recommend to everyone.

Examples:
- B12 for vegan diets;
- iron for confirmed iron deficiency;
- folic acid around conception;
- beta-alanine for repeated high-intensity efforts lasting roughly 0.5–10 min;
- sodium bicarbonate for selected high-intensity sports;
- calcium supplements when food intake cannot meet requirements.

### C — Promising but insufficient

Characteristics:
- some positive human RCT data;
- small/short studies, inconsistent findings or surrogate endpoints;
- unclear long-term safety or real-world importance.

Examples often include urolithin A, some collagen/tendon protocols, NMN/NR for specific biomarkers and certain herbal preparations for short-term symptoms.

### D — Little/no meaningful evidence for routine use

Characteristics:
- no consistent clinically important effect;
- redundant when diet/training is adequate;
- evidence mostly mechanistic or marketing-driven;
- effect is much smaller or less certain than cheaper alternatives.

Examples include BCAA for muscle gain when complete protein intake is already adequate, glutamine for routine athletic performance, most “testosterone boosters”, and most general-purpose greens powders.

### E — Avoid / poor risk-benefit

Characteristics:
- evidence of harm in the intended population;
- large contamination/adulteration risk;
- excessive doses with no plausible incremental benefit;
- stimulant or herbal blends with undisclosed quantities;
- products marketed with drug-like claims but without drug-level evidence/quality control.

Examples include beta-carotene supplements for smokers for disease prevention, high-dose vitamin E as a CVD/cancer preventive strategy, chronic unnecessary high-dose iron/iodine/zinc/selenium, and stimulant-heavy proprietary “fat burners”.

## 4. Deficiency correction is a different question from optimization

A nutrient can be essential and still provide no benefit when extra amounts are given to an already sufficient person.

Examples:
- Vitamin B12 supplementation is essential for many vegans and for true B12 deficiency. That does not imply that megadose B12 improves energy in a replete adult.
- Iron can dramatically improve symptoms/performance in iron deficiency. Unneeded iron creates GI effects and can be dangerous in overload states.
- Vitamin D treatment is appropriate for established indications and selected at-risk groups. Large preventive trials in generally healthy adults have not shown the broad fracture/CVD/cancer benefits commonly claimed online.

The relevant question is therefore not “is nutrient X important?” but **“is more of X beneficial in this person at this baseline status?”**

## 5. Relative risk vs absolute benefit

A relative risk reduction can sound much larger than the real-world benefit.

Example: if an intervention reduces risk from 2.0% to 1.6%, that is a 20% relative reduction but only a 0.4 percentage-point absolute reduction.

Whenever reliable absolute effects are available, they should be considered alongside relative effects.

## 6. Statistical significance vs practical significance

Very large meta-analyses can detect tiny effects that do not matter to an individual.

For performance supplements, a 1–2% improvement can matter greatly to a competitive athlete but may be irrelevant to a recreational lifter. For sleep, a statistically significant change of a few minutes may not justify cost, side effects or complexity.

## 7. Multiple ingredients make causal interpretation difficult

Proprietary stacks create several problems:
- the active dose of each ingredient may be unknown;
- duplicate vitamins/minerals can push total intake above upper limits;
- adverse reactions are harder to attribute;
- contamination risk is higher;
- studies on isolated ingredients do not validate the proprietary blend.

Default rule: **prefer a single ingredient with a known dose and known evidence**.

## 8. Food-first does not mean supplement-never

Food is preferred when it supplies the nutrient reliably because it also provides protein, fibre, minerals, phytochemicals and food structure. But supplements are rational when they are the easiest or safest way to meet a defined need.

Good examples:
- B12 in vegan diets;
- folic acid before/during early pregnancy;
- creatine for a performance goal;
- protein powder for convenience;
- psyllium to raise fibre intake;
- electrolyte/carbohydrate products during long endurance events.

## 9. Longevity claim standard

For this repository, a compound is **not** called life-extending in humans unless there is credible evidence of longer survival or reduced hard morbidity/mortality in humans in a relevant population.

As of 2026, popular compounds such as NMN, NR, resveratrol, spermidine, fisetin, quercetin and urolithin A do **not** meet that standard for healthy adults.

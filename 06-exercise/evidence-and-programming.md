# Exercise evidence and programming

## Bottom line

For health and longevity, the best-supported strategy is **both aerobic and resistance exercise**, plus high daily movement and little prolonged sedentary time. For muscle gain, resistance training is the main driver; for cardiorespiratory fitness and cardiovascular risk, aerobic training is the main driver. Neither fully replaces the other.

For a generally healthy adult, a strong default is:

- Resistance training **2–4 days/week**.
- Train each major muscle group about **2 times/week** when practical.
- Perform roughly **6–12 hard sets per muscle/week** as an effective, sustainable default; more can help hypertrophy, but returns diminish and recovery cost rises.
- Most hypertrophy work can use **5–30 reps/set** if sets are sufficiently hard. Moderate loads around **6–15 reps** are usually the most practical.
- For maximal strength, include heavier work, commonly **1–6 reps/set** with long rests.
- Most working sets should finish around **1–3 reps in reserve (RIR)**. Failure is not required.
- Use a controlled full range of motion that you can tolerate. Evidence increasingly favors loading muscles at longer lengths, but this is a refinement, not a requirement.
- Rest roughly **2–3 min for compound lifts** and **1–2+ min for isolation work**; longer is fine when needed to preserve performance.
- Progress load/reps over time rather than constantly changing exercises.

## What the 2026 ACSM position stand adds

The 2026 ACSM resistance-training position stand is an umbrella review synthesizing 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants. Its practical theme is that **consistent resistance training matters much more than small programming optimizations**.

Key conclusions:

- Almost any sensible resistance-training prescription improves strength and muscle compared with doing none.
- Multiple sets outperform one set on average.
- Heavier loads are more important for maximizing **1RM strength** than for hypertrophy.
- Muscle growth can occur across a broad range of loads when effort and total work are sufficient.
- Weekly set volume has a dose-response relationship with hypertrophy, but with diminishing returns.
- The exact “optimal” number of sets cannot be known for every person; the review notes diminishing returns around the high teens of weekly sets per muscle in meta-regression data.

## Repetitions and load

### Hypertrophy

Muscle can grow across a broad load range. A practical interpretation is:

- **5–10 reps:** efficient for combining strength + hypertrophy, but more joint/system fatigue per rep.
- **8–15 reps:** excellent general-purpose range.
- **15–30 reps:** also effective when sets are taken close enough to failure, but can be uncomfortable and time-consuming.

Very light loads require getting close to failure to recruit high-threshold motor units. Moderate loads are usually easier to standardize.

### Maximal strength

Heavier loads are superior for improving heavy 1RM performance because strength is specific to force production and the practiced loading range. Common programming uses:

- **1–6 reps/set**
- ~**80–95% 1RM** depending on the exercise and block
- **2–5 min rest** between hard sets

### Muscular endurance

Higher-repetition work is more specific to local muscular endurance. Sets of roughly **15–30+ reps** are commonly useful here.

## How close to failure?

Training to momentary muscular failure is **not necessary** for strength or hypertrophy.

A strong practical default:

- Compounds: stop around **1–3 RIR** most of the time.
- Isolation/machine work: **0–2 RIR** is usually tolerable.
- Beginners can grow well farther from failure while learning technique.
- Very low-load training usually needs to be closer to failure than moderate/heavy training.

A 2024 meta-regression found hypertrophy tended to improve as sets ended closer to failure, while strength showed little relationship with proximity to failure after accounting for other variables. This supports training hard without making every set an all-out failure set.

## Sets and weekly volume

### Minimum effective dose

- Even **1 hard set per exercise** can improve strength and muscle compared with no training.
- **2 sets per exercise** is a strong minimum default supported by the ACSM 2026 synthesis.

### Practical default

For most healthy adults:

- **6–12 hard sets per muscle/week** is a useful starting range.
- People prioritizing hypertrophy can often benefit from roughly **10–20 sets/week** for a target muscle if recovery and exercise quality remain good.
- More is not automatically better; diminishing returns become substantial at higher volumes.

Count sets by the muscle meaningfully trained. A bench press is a chest set and contributes to triceps/front-delt work, but not necessarily as much as direct isolation.

## Frequency

Frequency mainly helps distribute weekly volume and maintain set quality.

- **2×/week per muscle** is an excellent default.
- **1×/week** can still work if enough volume is done.
- **3×/week** can be useful for beginners, full-body plans, or high-volume muscles.

There is no requirement for a classic bodybuilding split.

## Rest intervals

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis found a small hypertrophy advantage for rest intervals above 60 seconds, with little clear additional hypertrophy benefit above about 90 seconds, though longer rest may still preserve strength/performance on demanding lifts.

Practical rule:

- Heavy compounds: **2–4 min**.
- Moderate hypertrophy compounds: **2–3 min**.
- Isolation/small-muscle work: **1–2 min**.
- If the next set loses many reps purely because you are breathless, rest longer.

## Range of motion and muscle length

Use the **largest pain-free range of motion you can control**. Full ROM is a robust default for strength and general function.

Recent evidence suggests training at **longer muscle lengths** may modestly favor hypertrophy in some muscles/regions. Examples include:

- Deep split squats rather than shallow reps.
- Overhead triceps extensions for the long head.
- Incline dumbbell curls for biceps at longer length.
- Romanian deadlifts for hamstrings.
- Calf raises with a deep stretch.

This is a refinement, not a reason to force painful end ranges.

## Tempo

There is no special hypertrophy magic in deliberately very slow reps.

Default:

- Lower under control, usually ~**1–3 s eccentric**.
- Reverse without bouncing.
- Lift with purposeful acceleration while maintaining form.

Extremely slow lifting usually reduces load and total productive work.

## Exercise order

Place the exercise you care most about first.

Typical priority:

1. Technical/heavy compound lift.
2. Second compound.
3. Machine/unilateral accessory.
4. Isolation work.
5. Core/carries if included.

If hypertrophy is the sole goal, exercise order matters less than maintaining high-quality hard sets.

## Progressive overload

Progression can come from:

- More reps with the same load.
- More load at the same reps.
- More ROM/control.
- An additional set when volume is too low.
- Harder bodyweight leverage.

A simple double-progression method:

1. Pick a range, e.g. 8–12 reps.
2. Keep the same load until all sets reach the top of the range at the target RIR.
3. Add ~2.5–5% load.
4. Repeat.

## Machines vs free weights

Both build muscle and strength. There is no general evidence that free weights are inherently superior for hypertrophy.

- Free weights: more stabilization, skill transfer to free-weight tasks, easy compound loading.
- Machines: stable, easy to learn, often easier to take close to failure safely.

Use whichever lets you train the target muscle hard, comfortably, and progressively.

## Unilateral vs bilateral

Both work. Unilateral training is useful for balance, asymmetries, and making lighter loads challenging. Bilateral training is efficient for heavy loading.

A mix is often ideal: squat/leg press plus split squat, bilateral row plus one-arm row, etc.

## Supersets, drop sets, rest-pause

These are mostly **time-efficiency tools**.

- Antagonist/non-competing supersets can save time with little downside.
- Drop sets/rest-pause can produce similar hypertrophy with less time but more discomfort/fatigue.
- Straight sets remain the simplest default.

## Periodization and deloading

For general health and modest muscle gain, complicated periodization is unnecessary. Progressing load/reps while managing fatigue is enough.

Deload when several signs persist together:

- Performance falling for >1 week.
- Unusual soreness/fatigue.
- Motivation and sleep deteriorating.
- Joint irritation accumulating.

A simple deload is 1 week with ~30–50% fewer hard sets and/or lower effort.

## Workout duration

There is no special physiological cutoff at 45, 60 or 90 minutes.

Typical efficient sessions:

- Full body: **45–75 min**.
- Upper/lower: **50–80 min**.
- Minimalist: **20–40 min**.

The right duration is the amount of time needed to complete high-quality work that you can recover from.

## Warm-up

- 5–10 min easy movement if desired.
- For the first heavy exercise, perform 2–4 progressively heavier warm-up sets.
- Dynamic movement is useful if a joint feels stiff.
- Long static stretching immediately before maximal strength/power work is unnecessary; if used, keep it brief.

## Recovery and safety

- Sleep: aim for a consistent **7–9 h/night** in most adults.
- Avoid large sudden jumps in load, volume, running mileage, or interval intensity.
- Mild muscular discomfort is normal; sharp joint pain, neurologic symptoms, chest pain, fainting, or unusual shortness of breath are not.
- People with cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, major injuries, pregnancy complications, or other significant medical conditions may need individualized clearance/programming.

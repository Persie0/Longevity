# Cardio vs strength: what is better?

## Short answer

For longevity and overall health, **doing both is better than choosing one**.

- Aerobic exercise is especially powerful for cardiorespiratory fitness, blood pressure, endothelial function, insulin sensitivity, and cardiovascular risk.
- Resistance training is especially powerful for strength, muscle mass, bone loading, function, glucose disposal, and protection against age-related frailty.
- Combined training covers more health domains than either alone.

WHO recommends adults perform **150–300 min/week of moderate aerobic activity, or 75–150 min/week vigorous activity, or an equivalent mix**, plus **muscle-strengthening exercise for all major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week**.

## Which gives the biggest longevity return?

The largest early benefit generally comes from moving from inactive to active. Aerobic activity has the deepest mortality evidence because cardiorespiratory fitness is strongly associated with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Resistance training adds independent value, especially through preserving muscle, strength, metabolic health, and function.

A practical longevity priority is therefore:

1. Avoid inactivity and prolonged sedentary time.
2. Accumulate regular moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity.
3. Strength train at least twice weekly.
4. Add some vigorous work if tolerated and desired.

## Aerobic targets

### Minimum guideline target

- **150 min/week moderate**, or
- **75 min/week vigorous**, or
- equivalent mixture.

### Strong practical target

For a healthy adult who enjoys training:

- **150–300 min/week moderate-equivalent aerobic activity**.
- Most of it easy/moderate.
- Optional 1–2 vigorous sessions/week.

More can provide extra benefit, though dose-response curves flatten at higher volumes.

## Intensity: Zone 2, moderate, and vigorous

Heart-rate zones vary by formula, so the **talk test** is often more practical.

### Easy / low intensity
- Comfortable conversation.
- Useful for steps, recovery, and accumulating activity.

### Moderate / “Zone 2-ish”
- Breathing noticeably harder but can still speak in sentences.
- Roughly 60–75% HRmax for many people, but individual thresholds vary.
- Excellent for sustainable weekly volume.

### Vigorous
- Conversation limited to short phrases.
- Running, hard cycling, rowing, swimming, or intervals.
- Improves VO2max efficiently.

You do not need to obsess over a device-defined Zone 2 boundary.

## Is HIIT necessary?

No.

HIIT is **time-efficient**, and can produce large VO2max improvements, but moderate continuous exercise can also substantially improve fitness and health.

Use HIIT if you enjoy it and recover well. A simple option is 1 session/week:

- 4 × 4 min hard with ~3 min easy recovery, or
- 6–10 × 1 min hard / 1–2 min easy.

For beginners, brisk walking, cycling, incline treadmill work, or easy running is enough initially.

## Walking and steps

Walking is one of the highest-value activities because it is accessible, low-risk, and easy to accumulate.

Rather than a magic step target:

- Increase from your current baseline.
- A practical target for many adults is roughly **7,000–10,000 steps/day**, but benefits begin below this and exact optimum varies.
- Break up long sitting periods with short walks.

## Cardio modality

Choose modalities you can repeat consistently.

### Excellent low-impact choices
- Cycling
- Incline walking
- Elliptical
- Rowing
- Swimming

### Running
Highly effective and time-efficient, but impact load is higher. Progress mileage gradually.

### Best choice
The modality that is safe for your joints, raises heart rate reliably, and you will continue doing.

## Strength vs cardio by outcome

| Outcome | Cardio | Strength | Best approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| VO2max / cardiorespiratory fitness | Excellent | Small-moderate | Cardio |
| Max strength | Small | Excellent | Strength |
| Muscle mass | Small | Excellent | Strength |
| Bone loading | Variable | Excellent with loading | Strength + impact if appropriate |
| Blood pressure | Excellent | Good | Both |
| Glucose control | Excellent | Excellent | Both |
| Body fat | Helpful | Helpful | Both + diet |
| Function in aging | Good | Excellent | Both + balance |
| Cardiovascular risk | Excellent | Good | Both |
| Frailty prevention | Helpful | Excellent | Strength + aerobic |
| Longevity | Strong evidence | Independent benefit | Both |

## Concurrent training and the “interference effect”

Cardio does not automatically kill muscle gains. The interference effect is usually small when programming is sensible.

To minimize interference when muscle/strength gain matters:

- Keep most cardio easy/moderate.
- Avoid very high volumes of hard endurance training.
- Separate hard cardio and hard leg lifting by **6+ hours** when possible, or put them on different days.
- If same session, do the modality that matches your priority first.
- Cycling generally interferes less with lower-body hypertrophy/strength than high-volume running.
- Eat enough calories and protein.

A longevity-focused person seeking only modest muscle gain can combine both without much concern.

## Sedentary behavior

Meeting exercise guidelines does not make prolonged sitting ideal.

Practical strategy:

- Stand or walk for a few minutes every 30–60 min when feasible.
- Use walking for short trips.
- Take 5–10 min walks after meals when convenient.

## Flexibility, mobility and balance

### Mobility/flexibility
Resistance training through a full comfortable ROM improves flexibility in many cases. Extra stretching is useful if a specific range is limited.

- 2–3 short sessions/week is enough for most goals.
- Hold stretches ~20–60 s and accumulate 1–3 rounds.

### Balance
Particularly important with aging.

- Single-leg stance
- Tandem stance/walk
- Step-downs
- Split squats
- Single-leg RDLs

Older adults should include multicomponent balance + strength work at least 3 days/week according to WHO guidance.

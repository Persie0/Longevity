# Supplements and powders: evidence-based decision guide

## Executive answer

For a healthy adult eating a good diet, **large supplement stacks are not supported by outcome evidence**. The default should be a small number of targeted products that solve a defined problem.

The most evidence-based “minimal stack” for a healthy resistance-training adult is often only:

1. **Creatine monohydrate** — if strength, repeated high-intensity performance and lean-mass gain matter.
2. **Protein powder** — only when useful to reach a sensible protein target.
3. **Psyllium** — optional when dietary fibre is low, constipation is present, or a modest additional LDL reduction is useful.
4. **Caffeine** — optional acute performance tool, not a health requirement.
5. **Specific vitamins/minerals only when dietary intake, life stage, deficiency risk or clinical testing supports them.**

A person who already eats adequate fish, dairy/fortified alternatives, iodized salt, varied plants and animal foods generally does not need to automatically add fish oil, a multivitamin, magnesium, zinc, vitamin C, vitamin E, B-complex, greens powder and several “longevity molecules”.

**No supplement currently has convincing randomized evidence that it extends lifespan in healthy humans.**

## Decision grades

- **A:** strong evidence for a defined goal; usually worthwhile if that goal applies.
- **B:** useful in a specific sport, deficiency, life stage or clinical context.
- **C:** promising but evidence is incomplete, inconsistent, short-term or surrogate-focused.
- **D:** little meaningful routine benefit / usually not worth buying.
- **E:** avoid or use only under specialist/medical supervision because risk-benefit is poor.

## Master decision table

| Supplement / powder | Grade | Best-supported use | Typical evidence-based use | Expected practical effect | Main caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Creatine monohydrate** | A | Strength, repeated high-intensity work, lean-mass support with resistance training | 3–5 g/day; optional loading 20 g/day split for 5–7 d | Meta-analyses show small additional lean-mass gain and meaningful extra strength vs training alone | Water-weight gain; serum creatinine may rise without GFR decline; kidney disease → clinician |
| **Protein powder** (whey/casein/soy/pea blend) | A/B | Convenience to hit total protein target | ~20–40 g serving as needed | Helpful when baseline protein is insufficient; little magic beyond adequate total protein | Food is equally valid; verify quality/allergens |
| **Caffeine** | A for performance | Endurance, alertness, some strength/power/endurance tasks | 1–3 mg/kg practical; strongest evidence 3–6 mg/kg ~30–60 min pre | Often a few percent performance benefit; highly individual | Sleep, anxiety, HR/BP, habituation; >9 mg/kg adds side effects without better performance |
| **Psyllium** | A/B | LDL lowering, fibre gap, constipation | ~7–10 g/day, titrate; plenty of water | ~10.2 g/day reduced LDL ~0.33 mmol/L in 28 RCTs | GI effects; choking/obstruction risk without water; separate from some medicines |
| **Beta-alanine** | B | High-intensity work ~0.5–10 min, repeated hard efforts | ~4–6.4 g/day split for ≥4 wk | Small average effect; greater for exercise capacity in relevant duration | Paresthesia; requires chronic loading, not an acute “pre-workout” dose |
| **Dietary nitrate / beetroot** | B | Endurance and some repeated/resistance performance | Commonly ~5–9 mmol nitrate (~300–600 mg) 2–3 h pre | Small average performance improvement; responders/nonresponders | Product nitrate content varies; antibacterial mouthwash may blunt nitrate→nitrite conversion |
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | B | High-intensity events / repeated bouts | 0.2–0.3 g/kg, usually 60–180 min pre | Reproducible ergogenic effect in suitable events | GI distress common; large sodium load; individualize timing |
| **Carbohydrate drink/gel/powder** | B | Prolonged endurance / high-volume training | Often ~30–90 g carbohydrate/hour depending duration/tolerance | Maintains carbohydrate availability and performance in long exercise | Unnecessary for short normal gym sessions; dental/GI considerations |
| **Electrolyte/sodium products** | B | Long/hot/sweaty exercise or large sweat losses | Individualized to sweat rate/duration | Helps replace fluid/sodium losses | Not a daily health supplement for sedentary people; hypertension/kidney issues matter |
| **Vitamin B12** | A/B when indicated | Vegan diet, deficiency/malabsorption risk, some older adults | Reliable fortified foods/supplement; regimen depends dose/frequency | Prevents/treats deficiency and neurological/hematological consequences | “Energy booster” claims in replete people unsupported |
| **Iron** | A/B when deficient | Iron deficiency / iron-deficiency anemia | Dose determined by diagnosis and clinician | Can substantially improve anemia, fatigue and performance when deficient | Do not take blindly; GI effects and iron overload; adult UL 45 mg/day for routine intake |
| **Folic acid** | A/B | Periconception/pregnancy neural-tube-defect prevention | 400–800 mcg/day around conception in standard-risk guidance | Strong preventive benefit | High-dose folic acid can mask B12 deficiency; special high-risk dosing is medical |
| **Vitamin D** | B | Meeting intake needs, selected at-risk groups/established indications, adults >75 per Endocrine Society guidance | RDA 600 IU/day through age 70; 800 IU >70 in US DRI; individualized if treating deficiency | Broad preventive benefits are not demonstrated in replete healthy adults; >75 may have mortality benefit signal | Routine high-dose use/testing not recommended in healthy <75 without indication |
| **Calcium** | B | Close a real dietary gap; bone-health plans when intake insufficient | Food first; supplement only the gap | Essential nutrient, but more is not automatically better | Kidney stone concerns; interactions; total UL 2,000–2,500 mg/day adults by age |
| **Iodine** | B | Low iodine intake, especially vegan/low dairy/fish/no iodized salt | Aim around RDA 150 mcg/day adult total intake | Prevents deficiency/thyroid consequences | Excess also causes thyroid dysfunction; adult UL 1,100 mcg/day (US) |
| **Omega-3 EPA/DHA** | B/C for healthy primary prevention | Low-fish diets; specific clinical indications at prescription doses | Prefer 2 fish meals/week; supplement dose depends goal | 1 g/day did not reduce VITAL composite CVD endpoint; some MI signal; high-dose prescription EPA is a different clinical use | Higher-dose omega-3 can increase atrial-fibrillation risk; bleeding/drug context |

| **Magnesium** | B if intake/status low; C for sleep | Deficiency/intake gap; some clinical uses | Usually modest supplemental amounts; US supplemental UL 350 mg/day | Corrects deficiency; sleep evidence is low/very-low certainty | Diarrhea; kidney disease; antibiotic/bisphosphonate interactions |

| **Selenium** | B when deficient; D routinely | Deficiency is uncommon in many regions | Usually food-first | Essential trace nutrient | Narrower safety margin; excess causes selenosis; no routine anti-aging benefit |

| **Spermidine** | C/D | Research compound | — | 12-mo RCT at 0.9 mg/day did not improve primary memory endpoint | Human longevity benefit unproven |
| **Urolithin A** | C | Research/older-adult muscle-endurance signal | 1,000 mg/day in one 4-mo older-adult RCT | Some endurance/biomarker improvements; primary 6-min-walk/ATP endpoints not clearly improved | Small, short, industry-linked field; no longevity outcome |
| **Resveratrol** | D/C | Research/metabolic endpoints | — | Human results inconsistent; no lifespan extension evidence | Drug interactions; low bioavailability; marketing exceeds data |
| **NAC** | B medically; D/C wellness | Acetaminophen antidote/mucolytic medically; wellness claims weak | Clinical dose depends indication | Clear medical uses, limited routine exercise/longevity evidence | Drug interactions, GI effects; not a proven anti-aging supplement |
| **Green-tea extract / high-dose EGCG** | D/E at high dose | No need if drinking tea | — | Tea beverage is reasonable; concentrated extract not proven to improve longevity | EFSA: ≥800 mg EGCG/day from supplements linked to elevated liver enzymes; rare liver injury below that also reported |
| **Vitamin E high-dose for prevention** | E/D | Not recommended for CVD/cancer prevention | — | USPSTF: no net preventive benefit | Bleeding/hemorrhagic-stroke concerns at high doses |
| **Beta-carotene supplement in smokers** | E | None for CVD/cancer prevention | — | Increased lung-cancer risk in high-risk smokers/asbestos-exposed groups | Use food carotenoids instead |

| **Proprietary stimulant pre-workouts/fat burners** | E/D | Avoid when ingredients/doses unclear | — | May improve alertness if caffeine present, but transparent caffeine is simpler | Mislabeling/adulteration, high stimulant load, cardiovascular/sleep risk |

## What this table means in practice

### Strong evidence does not mean mandatory

Creatine works, but someone with no interest in resistance performance does not need it. Caffeine works, but if it worsens sleep, the net effect can be negative.

### Weak longevity evidence does not mean a compound is biologically inert

NMN can raise NAD-related metabolites. Berberine can lower glucose. Curcumin can improve osteoarthritis symptoms. The point is that **none of those findings automatically means a healthy person will live longer by taking the product**.

### Correcting deficiency can have a large effect

B12, iron, iodine, folate, vitamin D, calcium, zinc and other nutrients can be extremely important when intake/status is inadequate. This is fundamentally different from adding large doses on top of sufficiency.

## Minimal buying rule

Before buying a supplement, answer all five:

1. **What exact outcome am I targeting?**
2. **Is there randomized human evidence for that outcome?**
3. **Am I deficient or in a subgroup that benefits?**
4. **What dose was actually studied?**
5. **Could I get the same benefit more reliably from food, training, sleep or proven medical treatment?**

If the answers are vague, the product is probably not a high-value purchase.

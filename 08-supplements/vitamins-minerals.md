# Vitamins and minerals: when supplements actually make sense

## The central principle

An essential nutrient can have a dramatic effect when someone is deficient and little or no benefit when the same nutrient is added on top of an already sufficient diet.

This is why the useful question is not “is vitamin D/B12/magnesium important?” It is:

> **Is this person likely to be insufficient, and is supplementation shown to improve an outcome at that baseline status?**

For generally healthy adults without known deficiencies, routine megadose vitamin/mineral supplementation has not demonstrated broad reductions in cardiovascular disease, cancer or mortality.

The 2022 USPSTF evidence review included 84 studies and 739,803 participants. The Task Force concluded:
- evidence was insufficient to determine net benefit for multivitamins or most single nutrients for prevention of CVD/cancer;
- vitamin E had no net preventive benefit;
- beta-carotene harms outweighed benefits for prevention, particularly because lung-cancer risk increased in high-risk smokers/asbestos-exposed groups.

Source: https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/vitamin-supplementation-to-prevent-cvd-and-cancer-preventive-medication

## 1. Vitamin D — Grade B when context warrants it

### What is established

Vitamin D is essential for calcium/phosphate metabolism and bone health. Severe deficiency matters. The controversy is whether generally healthy, vitamin-D-replete adults should take doses above normal dietary recommendations to prevent broad chronic disease.

### 2024 Endocrine Society prevention guideline

For generally healthy people without established treatment indications:

- **<50 years:** suggests against routine vitamin D supplementation above the Dietary Reference Intake and against routine 25(OH)D screening.
- **50–74 years:** suggests against routine empiric supplementation above the DRI and against routine screening.
- **≥75 years:** suggests empiric vitamin D because trial evidence indicates potential mortality benefit; daily lower-dose regimens are preferred. Across included mortality trials, doses ranged 400–3,333 IU/day equivalent, with a weighted average around **900 IU/day**.
- pregnancy and high-risk prediabetes are separate populations where the guideline suggests empiric supplementation.

US RDA cited by the guideline:
- 600 IU (15 mcg)/day through age 70;
- 800 IU (20 mcg)/day above 70.

Source: https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/vitamin-d-for-prevention-of-disease

### Large-trial reality check

The VITAL fracture ancillary trial randomized generally healthy adults to vitamin D3 **2,000 IU/day** or placebo. It did **not** significantly reduce:
- total fractures: HR ~0.98;
- nonvertebral fractures: HR ~0.97;
- hip fractures: HR ~1.01.

Source: NEJM 2022, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2202106.

This does not mean vitamin D never matters. It means giving substantial vitamin D to an unselected generally healthy population is not the same as treating deficiency/osteoporosis risk.

### When testing is more useful

Routine screening in healthy adults is not recommended by the Endocrine Society, but 25(OH)D testing can be relevant in clinical situations such as:
- metabolic bone disease/osteoporosis evaluation;
- malabsorption;
- hypocalcemia;
- certain kidney/liver/endocrine disorders;
- suspected severe deficiency;
- clinician-guided treatment.

### Safety

The US adult UL is commonly **4,000 IU/day (100 mcg)** for unsupervised total intake. Toxicity from excessive supplementation can cause hypercalcemia, kidney stones/injury, soft-tissue calcification and arrhythmia.

High-dose intermittent “bolus” strategies are not a default longevity practice.

## 2. Vitamin B12 — Grade A/B when diet/status creates risk

### Why it is different

B12 is one of the clearest supplements for a plant-based diet because reliable natural dietary sources are overwhelmingly animal-derived; fortified foods/supplements solve the problem safely and cheaply.

Adult RDA: **2.4 mcg/day**.

Source: NIH ODS Vitamin B12 fact sheet: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/

### Higher-risk groups

- vegans and some vegetarians;
- pernicious anemia;
- gastric bypass/gastrectomy;
- Crohn’s/celiac or other malabsorption disorders;
- older adults with reduced food-bound B12 absorption;
- prolonged metformin use;
- prolonged acid-suppressing therapy in some people.

### Important practical point

The RDA is the amount needed after normal absorption. Oral supplement tablets commonly contain much more because fractional/passive absorption is low at high doses.

There is no need to interpret a high supplemental dose as evidence of a special pharmacologic benefit. Its job is to guarantee adequate absorbed B12.

### Testing

Useful when deficiency is plausible:
- serum B12;
- methylmalonic acid (MMA), especially for borderline cases;
- homocysteine can contribute context but is less specific.

### What B12 does *not* do

In a person with adequate B12, extra B12 is not a proven stimulant, nootropic or longevity intervention.

## 3. Folate / folic acid — Grade A/B around pregnancy, otherwise food first

Adult RDA: **400 mcg DFE/day**. Pregnancy RDA is higher.

The strongest supplement use is neural-tube-defect prevention.

US guidance summarized by NIH ODS/USPSTF:
- people planning or capable of pregnancy should generally receive **400–800 mcg/day folic acid**, beginning before conception and through early pregnancy in standard-risk situations.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Folate-HealthProfessional/

### Why food folate and folic acid are not numerically identical

Folic acid in supplements/fortified foods is more bioavailable than natural food folate, which is why dietary folate equivalents (DFE) are used.

### Safety

Large folic-acid doses can correct the anemia of B12 deficiency while neurological B12 injury continues. Do not use high-dose folic acid as a general wellness strategy without understanding B12 status and indication.

Previous neural-tube-defect pregnancy or certain high-risk medications may require clinician-directed high-dose folic acid; that is not a general supplement recommendation.

## 4. Iron — Grade A when deficient, D/E when taken blindly

### Why deficiency matters

Iron deficiency can reduce:
- hemoglobin/oxygen transport;
- exercise performance;
- cognition/concentration;
- energy and wellbeing;
- hair/nail health in some cases.

Treatment can be highly effective **when deficiency exists**.

### Why routine supplementation is a bad idea

The body has limited regulated iron excretion. Excess iron can accumulate, and hereditary hemochromatosis is common enough that long-term blind supplementation is inappropriate.

NIH ODS adult UL for routine intake: **45 mg/day**.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-Consumer/

### Who is at higher risk of deficiency

- menstruating people, especially heavy bleeding;
- pregnancy;
- endurance athletes, particularly female runners;
- frequent blood donation;
- low-iron vegan/vegetarian diets;
- GI blood loss;
- celiac/IBD and malabsorption;
- bariatric surgery.

### Testing

Do not diagnose from fatigue alone. Common workup includes:
- CBC/hemoglobin;
- ferritin;
- transferrin saturation;
- CRP/inflammation context because ferritin is an acute-phase reactant.

### Safety

Iron commonly causes constipation, nausea and abdominal pain. Large acute doses are dangerous and iron tablets are a serious poisoning risk for children.

Hemochromatosis/iron overload: avoid iron supplements unless specifically prescribed.

## 5. Iodine — Grade B when diet is unreliable

Adult US RDA: **150 mcg/day**.

Adult US UL: **1,100 mcg/day**.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iodine-HealthProfessional/

### Dietary sources

Major sources depend on country:
- iodized salt;
- dairy (partly due agricultural/processing practices);
- fish/seafood;
- eggs;
- seaweed, which can contain extremely variable amounts.

### Higher-risk pattern

A vegan who avoids iodized salt and rarely eats seaweed/fortified foods can have low intake. In that situation, a modest standardized iodine supplement can be much safer than trying to obtain iodine from kelp with unpredictable dosing.

### Excess is not better

High iodine intake can trigger hypo- or hyperthyroidism in susceptible people. “Thyroid support” products containing kelp/iodine are a common way to overshoot.

Pregnancy/lactation iodine requirements differ and should follow obstetric guidance.

## 6. Calcium — Grade B to fill a gap

### Requirements

NIH ODS adult RDAs:
- age 19–50: **1,000 mg/day**;
- men 51–70: 1,000 mg/day;
- women 51–70: **1,200 mg/day**;
- >70: **1,200 mg/day**.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/

### Food-first sources

- yogurt/milk/kefir;
- calcium-fortified soy milk;
- calcium-set tofu;
- sardines/salmon with bones;
- kale/bok choy/broccoli;
- some mineral waters.

### When supplements help

If habitual diet is clearly below requirement and it is impractical to fix with food, supplement **the gap**, not automatically 1,000+ mg on top of the diet.

### Upper limits

NIH ODS:
- age 19–50: **2,500 mg/day total**;
- age 51+: **2,000 mg/day total**.

Potential issues include kidney stones and medication interactions. Calcium can interfere with levothyroxine, some antibiotics and dolutegravir; timing separation may be required.

## 7. Magnesium — Grade B for deficiency/intake gap, C for sleep

### Dietary role

Magnesium is abundant in:
- nuts/seeds;
- legumes;
- whole grains;
- leafy greens;
- cocoa.

A Mediterranean-style diet can supply substantial magnesium without supplements.

### Sleep claims

A 2021 meta-analysis in older adults with insomnia found only **3 RCTs / 151 participants**. Sleep-onset latency was about **17 minutes shorter**, but certainty was low/very low and other outcomes were uncertain.

Source: PMID 33865376, DOI 10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z.

This is not strong evidence for universal “magnesium before bed”.

### Supplemental upper limit

US UL is **350 mg/day from supplements/medications**, not including magnesium naturally present in food.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/

### Forms

Magnesium oxide contains a high percentage of elemental magnesium but is often less well absorbed and more laxative. Citrate/glycinate are commonly better tolerated, but marketing claims that one form uniquely improves brain/sleep outcomes are usually much stronger than comparative clinical evidence.

### Safety/interactions

- diarrhea/cramps;
- severe toxicity can occur with renal failure or massive intake;
- separate from tetracycline/quinolone antibiotics;
- separate from oral bisphosphonates;
- diuretics/PPIs can affect magnesium status.

Kidney disease → do not supplement casually.

## 8. Zinc — Grade B when deficient, D for chronic high-dose wellness use

Adult US UL: **40 mg/day**.

Source: NIH ODS: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Zinc-HealthProfessional/

### Food sources

- oysters/shellfish;
- meat/dairy;
- pumpkin seeds;
- legumes/whole grains (absorption lower because phytate binds zinc).

### Who can be at risk

- very restricted diets;
- malabsorption;
- some vegan diets with low total zinc;
- alcoholism/clinical conditions.

### Why chronic high doses are a bad idea

Excess zinc can cause:
- nausea/GI symptoms;
- **copper deficiency**;
- anemia/neurological problems secondary to low copper;
- reduced HDL at very high chronic doses.

Copper deficiency has been observed with zinc intakes around 60 mg/day in studies; this is why the adult UL is 40 mg/day.

Zinc also interacts with quinolone/tetracycline antibiotics and penicillamine.

## 9. Selenium — Grade B only when intake is actually low

Adult RDA: typically **55 mcg/day** in US guidance.

Adult UL: **400 mcg/day**.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Selenium-HealthProfessional/

### Food sources

- seafood;
- eggs/meat/dairy;
- grains depending soil;
- Brazil nuts can contain very high and highly variable selenium.

### Why more is not better

Selenosis can cause:
- hair/nail brittleness/loss;
- garlic breath/metallic taste;
- GI and neurological symptoms;
- severe toxicity at extreme exposure.

Do not use large daily Brazil-nut quantities or high-dose selenium as an “antioxidant longevity” strategy.

## 10. Vitamin K / K2 — Grade C/D for routine anti-aging use

Vitamin K is essential for clotting and participates in bone-related proteins. K1 is abundant in leafy greens; K2 forms occur in fermented foods and animal products.

What is **not established**:
- that routine K2 supplementation extends life;
- that it prevents cardiovascular disease in healthy adults sufficiently to justify universal use;
- that everyone taking vitamin D needs a K2 pill.

No UL has been established because toxicity is low in healthy people.

### Critical interaction

Vitamin K can have a **serious interaction with warfarin and related vitamin-K-antagonist anticoagulants**. Consistency of intake matters. Do not start/stop K supplements without anticoagulation guidance.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/

## 11. Vitamin C — Grade D for routine megadosing

Vitamin C is essential, but fruits and vegetables make deficiency uncommon in a varied diet.

High-dose supplementation has not demonstrated broad longevity/CVD/cancer prevention benefits in replete adults.

Adult UL: **2,000 mg/day**.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-HealthProfessional/

High doses can cause:
- diarrhea/cramps;
- increased urinary oxalate/kidney-stone concern in susceptible people;
- worsening iron overload in hereditary hemochromatosis.

Food sources—peppers, citrus, kiwi, berries, broccoli, cabbage—are preferred.

## 12. Vitamin E — Grade D/E as a prevention supplement

Vitamin E is naturally available in nuts, seeds and plant oils.

USPSTF concludes there is **no net benefit** from vitamin E supplementation for prevention of cardiovascular disease or cancer in community-dwelling adults.

High supplemental doses can inhibit platelet aggregation and increase bleeding risk; hemorrhagic stroke is a concern.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminE-HealthProfessional/

Verdict: get vitamin E from foods; do not use high-dose vitamin E as an anti-aging strategy.

## 13. Vitamin A and beta-carotene — food first; high-dose supplements can harm

Preformed vitamin A (retinol) is essential but accumulates and can be toxic at high doses. Pregnancy is especially sensitive because excess preformed vitamin A is teratogenic.

### Beta-carotene and smokers

Large trials found increased lung cancer in smokers receiving high-dose beta-carotene. In the ATBC trial, **20 mg/day beta-carotene increased lung-cancer risk by about 18%** in male smokers.

Source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminA-HealthProfessional/

USPSTF therefore recommends against beta-carotene supplementation for CVD/cancer prevention.

Carrots, leafy greens and other carotenoid-rich foods do not carry the same warning.

## 14. B-complex products — usually Grade D unless a component is indicated

“B vitamins help energy metabolism” is true biochemistry but does not mean extra B vitamins make a replete person feel more energetic.

Potential problems:
- unnecessary expense;
- very high B6 doses can cause peripheral neuropathy;
- high folic acid can obscure B12 deficiency;
- niacin at pharmacologic doses causes flushing/hepatotoxicity and should not be treated as a simple wellness vitamin;
- multiple fortified foods + multivitamin + B-complex can create huge duplicate doses.

Better approach: identify the specific nutrient risk (B12, folate, etc.) and supplement it directly.

## 15. Multivitamins — Grade B/C as a gap-filler, not a lifespan pill

### What large evidence reviews say

USPSTF: insufficient evidence to conclude multivitamins prevent CVD/cancer. Its evidence review found a small reduction in overall cancer incidence (pooled OR ~0.93), but with important limitations and no clear broad mortality benefit.

### COSMOS cognition signal

In older adults, the COSMOS cognitive substudy/meta-analysis across three cognitive substudies found small improvements:
- global cognition ~**0.07 SD**;
- episodic memory ~**0.06 SD**.

Authors described the magnitude as roughly equivalent to two years less cognitive aging, but this is a small test-score effect in older adults—not proof of dementia prevention or longer life.

Source: American Journal of Clinical Nutrition 2024, DOI 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2023.12.011.

### Who might reasonably use one

- older adult with inconsistent diet;
- calorie-restricted diet;
- broad food exclusions;
- travel/food-access situations;
- clinician/dietitian identifies several small gaps.

### What product to choose

Prefer:
- doses near ~100% of daily requirements, not 1,000–10,000%;
- no proprietary herb blend;
- little/no iron unless iron is appropriate for you;
- no large beta-carotene/retinol dose in smokers/former smokers;
- credible quality verification.

## 16. Lab testing: when it is worth more than guessing

Labs can be useful when the result changes management.

Common examples:
- **iron:** CBC, ferritin, transferrin saturation;
- **B12:** B12 ± MMA;
- **vitamin D:** only when clinical indication/risk justifies it, not universal wellness screening;
- **thyroid:** TSH/free T4 when iodine/thyroid disease is a concern rather than taking “thyroid support” blindly;
- **electrolytes/renal function:** if kidney disease/medications make mineral supplementation risky.

The best supplement strategy is often **measure → correct → reassess**, not indefinitely stacking pills.

# Longevity and wellness compounds: what human evidence actually shows

## Bottom line

Most marketed “longevity” supplements have **biological plausibility without proof that they prolong healthy human life**. Many improve a laboratory marker, increase a metabolite, or show effects in mice. That is not equivalent to fewer heart attacks, less disability, lower mortality or longer lifespan in healthy humans.

## NAD+ boosters: NR and NMN

Nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) reliably increase circulating NAD-related metabolites in many short trials. This confirms **target engagement**, not anti-aging efficacy.

Human trials have generally been small and short. Reported outcomes include insulin sensitivity, vascular function, exercise capacity, muscle parameters and inflammatory markers, but results are inconsistent and effect sizes modest. There is no trial showing that NR or NMN extends human lifespan or prevents major age-related disease in healthy adults.

Practical conclusion: **interesting research tools, not established longevity medicines**. Cost is high relative to demonstrated benefit.

## Resveratrol

Resveratrol became popular from sirtuin and caloric-restriction pathways. Human data have not matched the enthusiasm generated by animal/mechanistic work.

Findings across trials are inconsistent for glucose control, blood pressure, lipids, inflammation and exercise adaptation. Bioavailability is poor and doses/formulations vary. There is no evidence that routine supplementation prolongs human life.

High antioxidant/polyphenol doses around exercise may theoretically blunt some training adaptations, though evidence is inconsistent. Food sources such as berries, grapes and peanuts are preferable to chasing pharmacologic resveratrol doses.

## Spermidine

Spermidine is involved in autophagy and cellular homeostasis. Higher dietary intake has been associated observationally with better outcomes, but confounding is difficult to exclude because spermidine-rich diets are also rich in whole grains, legumes, mushrooms and other healthy foods.

Small intervention studies have examined cognition and biomarkers. Results are not strong enough to recommend a supplement for longevity. A food-first approach is easy: whole grains, legumes, mushrooms, nuts/seeds and fermented foods.

## Urolithin A

Urolithin A is a metabolite generated from ellagitannins by some gut microbiomes. Supplementation bypasses microbiome variability. Small randomized trials in older/middle-aged adults suggest improvements in selected mitochondrial biomarkers and some muscle endurance/function outcomes.

This is one of the more interesting emerging compounds, but the evidence is still **surrogate/function oriented**, not disease- or lifespan-outcome evidence. It remains optional/experimental rather than baseline care.

## Berberine

Berberine has pharmacologic effects on glucose and lipid metabolism. Meta-analyses suggest reductions in fasting glucose, HbA1c, triglycerides and LDL in people with metabolic abnormalities.

Important distinction: this is closer to a **drug-like botanical** than a general wellness supplement.

Risks/limitations:

- GI side effects are common.
- It can interact with CYP enzymes, P-glycoprotein and many medications.
- Product quality varies.
- Pregnancy/breastfeeding use is inappropriate without medical oversight.
- Evidence does not show lifespan extension in healthy metabolically normal adults.

Do not use it to self-treat diabetes instead of evidence-based medical care.

## Curcumin / turmeric extracts

Curcumin has anti-inflammatory effects in vitro and modest evidence for pain reduction in osteoarthritis and some inflammatory markers. Oral bioavailability is low; formulations with piperine or lipid carriers change exposure and interaction potential.

Potential issues:

- GI upset;
- gallbladder/biliary issues in susceptible people;
- bleeding/drug-interaction concerns;
- rare liver injury reports, particularly with concentrated extracts.

Using turmeric as a food/spice is low risk. Concentrated capsules are not proven longevity treatments.

## Coenzyme Q10

CoQ10 participates in mitochondrial electron transport. Evidence is most relevant in selected clinical populations, including some heart-failure contexts and possibly statin-associated muscle symptoms in subsets, though results are mixed for the latter.

Routine supplementation in a healthy person with no indication has not been shown to extend life. Typical supplemental doses in trials are roughly **100–300 mg/day**.

Warfarin interaction is possible.

## Ashwagandha

Small randomized trials suggest possible improvements in perceived stress, sleep and in some studies strength/testosterone-related outcomes. Effects are not large or universally replicated.

Safety deserves more attention than marketing implies:

- GI upset and sedation can occur;
- thyroid hormone changes have been reported;
- rare liver injury cases exist;
- avoid in pregnancy;
- caution with thyroid, sedative, immunosuppressive and other medications.

Use standardized, third-party tested extracts if used at all.

## Rhodiola rosea

Evidence suggests possible reductions in fatigue/stress and modest performance benefits, but studies are heterogeneous and often small. It is not established for longevity.

## Lion’s mane

Small human trials have reported signals for cognition, mood or mild cognitive impairment, but sample sizes are small and products/doses vary. No robust evidence supports prevention of dementia or longer life.

## Glycine

Glycine participates in collagen, glutathione and neurotransmission. Small trials suggest possible sleep-quality and metabolic effects. Human longevity claims largely rely on mechanisms and indirect evidence.

Typical supplemental use is **~3 g before bed** in sleep studies. This is a low-cost experimental option, not a proven health-span intervention.

## Taurine

Taurine is abundant in many tissues and has roles in osmoregulation, bile acids and muscle/neuronal function. Animal work generated major longevity interest, but human evidence does not establish that supplementation extends lifespan.

Some trials suggest modest exercise-performance or cardiometabolic effects. Doses commonly studied are **1–3 g/day**. More outcome data are needed.

## NAC

N-acetylcysteine is a glutathione precursor and an established medication for acetaminophen poisoning and selected respiratory indications. That does not make it a routine longevity supplement.

Possible roles include oxidative-stress and respiratory endpoints, but long-term healthy-adult benefits are uncertain. It can cause nausea and interact with nitroglycerin and other drugs.

## Alpha-lipoic acid

ALA has evidence in diabetic neuropathy and some glucose/oxidative-stress outcomes. Routine use in healthy adults is not supported for longevity. It can lower glucose and can interact with diabetes/thyroid therapies.

## Quercetin and fisetin

These flavonoids are marketed as “senolytics.” Human evidence for clearing senescent cells in a way that improves clinical outcomes is inadequate.

Fisetin in particular remains largely a preclinical/early-trial concept. Until large controlled human trials show meaningful benefits, senolytic stacks should be considered experimental.

## PQQ

Pyrroloquinoline quinone has mitochondrial marketing claims but very limited high-quality human data. No convincing reason for routine use.

## Astaxanthin

Trials have studied skin, eye, exercise and oxidative-stress outcomes with small or inconsistent effects. It is not a proven longevity supplement.

## Green tea extract / EGCG

Drinking tea is generally compatible with healthy dietary patterns. Concentrated green-tea extracts are different: high-dose EGCG can cause liver injury, particularly when taken fasting.

If the goal is health, drinking unsweetened green tea is usually the lower-risk choice.

## Melatonin

Melatonin is best understood as a **circadian signal**, not a sedative longevity pill.

Good uses include:

- jet lag;
- circadian phase shifting;
- selected sleep-onset problems, especially in older adults or delayed schedules.

Small doses often work: **0.3–1 mg** can be enough for circadian purposes; 1–3 mg is commonly used. Larger doses often add next-day grogginess without greater benefit.

Product content can vary widely, especially in gummies. Prefer verified products. Long-term outcome data do not show lifespan extension.

## “Testosterone boosters”

Most combinations of tribulus, fenugreek, D-aspartic acid, tongkat-style blends and proprietary herbal stacks do not produce reliable clinically meaningful testosterone increases in eugonadal men.

If symptoms of hypogonadism exist, measure morning testosterone appropriately and investigate sleep, obesity, medications, illness and pituitary/testicular causes rather than buying a booster.

Products marketed for sexual performance/bodybuilding have higher adulteration risk with undeclared drugs or steroid-like compounds.

## What not to infer from biomarker studies

The following findings can be scientifically interesting but are **not proof of longer life**:

- increased NAD+;
- lower CRP;
- increased glutathione;
- lower fasting glucose for a few weeks;
- altered microbiome diversity;
- improved mitochondrial gene expression;
- reduced oxidative-stress markers;
- epigenetic-age shifts without validated clinical consequences.

A supplement deserves a strong recommendation only when the outcome that matters to the user is actually improved with acceptable risk.

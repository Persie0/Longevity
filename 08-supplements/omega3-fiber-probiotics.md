# Omega-3, fibre, probiotics and gut-health supplements

## Decision summary

| Intervention | Practical evidence rating | Main evidence-backed use | Routine use in a healthy well-nourished adult? |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Psyllium husk | A/B | LDL reduction, constipation, fibre gap, modest glycaemic benefit | Often reasonable if diet is short on soluble fibre |
| EPA/DHA from food | A | Cardiovascular nutrition, triglycerides, protein/nutrients from seafood | Prefer 2–3 seafood meals/week |
| Prescription/high-dose omega-3 | B | Selected hypertriglyceridaemia/CV indications under medical care | No |
| OTC fish oil | B/C | Fills EPA/DHA gap; modest TG lowering | Optional, not a universal longevity supplement |
| Algal EPA/DHA | B | Vegan/vegetarian EPA/DHA source | Useful when seafood is absent |
| Prebiotic fibres | B | Stool frequency, selected GI effects | Prefer food first; useful selectively |
| Probiotics | B/C | Strain-specific GI indications | Not a generic daily longevity requirement |
| Synbiotics | C | Selected GI/metabolic endpoints | Evidence product/strain specific |
| Greens powders | D | Convenience/micronutrient marketing | Do not substitute for vegetables/fruit |

## Omega-3: food vs capsules

### What is established

EPA and DHA lower triglycerides, influence cell membranes and inflammatory signalling, and are essential/conditionally essential long-chain omega-3 fats. Fish consumption is consistently associated with lower cardiovascular risk, but this does **not** mean every fish-oil capsule reduces events in every healthy person.

The strongest practical recommendation remains food-first:

- eat **2–3 seafood meals/week**;
- emphasize low-mercury oily fish such as salmon, sardines, herring, trout and Atlantic/Pacific chub mackerel;
- use tuna mainly as a convenient lean protein rather than the primary omega-3 source.

### OTC fish oil

For a healthy person without high triglycerides or a specific clinical indication, randomized trials of generic EPA+DHA supplements have not shown a consistent large reduction in major cardiovascular events. Effects are more compelling for lowering triglycerides than for extending life.

A common supplemental range is **~500–1,000 mg/day combined EPA+DHA** when seafood intake is low. This is a dietary-gap strategy, not a proven anti-aging dose.

### High-dose EPA and prescription products

Several grams/day of purified omega-3 can substantially lower triglycerides. Selected prescription preparations have outcome data in high-risk patients, but results vary by formulation and trial population. This should not be extrapolated into a recommendation for healthy adults to take multi-gram OTC fish oil.

### Safety

- Fishy aftertaste/GI upset are common.
- Multi-gram doses can increase bleeding tendency modestly and, in some large trials, were associated with more atrial fibrillation.
- People using anticoagulants/antiplatelets, with arrhythmia history, or preparing for procedures should discuss high-dose use with a clinician.
- Oxidation and mislabeling are quality concerns; prefer reputable third-party tested products.

### Algal omega-3

Algae-derived DHA/EPA is a useful vegan substitute. It is especially relevant when a diet contains little/no fish and little conversion from ALA is expected. Typical practical intake: **250–500 mg/day EPA+DHA equivalent**, adjusted to diet and clinical context.

## ALA from plants

Flax, chia, walnuts, hemp and canola provide alpha-linolenic acid (ALA). ALA is beneficial as part of a healthy diet, but conversion to EPA—and especially DHA—is limited and variable. Plant foods should still be eaten; they simply do not guarantee the same long-chain omega-3 exposure as fish/algae.

## Psyllium: one of the highest-value powders

Psyllium is a viscous soluble fibre with reproducible benefits.

### Effects

Evidence supports:

- **LDL-C reduction**, often roughly 5–10% depending on dose/background diet;
- improved stool consistency/frequency in constipation;
- modest reductions in post-meal glucose and HbA1c, especially when baseline glycaemic control is poor;
- increased total fibre intake without much energy.

### Dose

A practical range is **5 g once or twice daily**, often totaling **~7–12 g/day** in trials. Start lower and increase gradually.

### How to take it

- Mix with a large glass of water and drink promptly.
- Follow with additional fluid.
- Separate from some medications by ~2 hours because fibre can alter absorption.
- Do not take dry powder without adequate liquid.

Avoid/use medical guidance with swallowing disorders, bowel obstruction/strictures or severe unexplained GI symptoms.

## Other fibre powders

### Inulin / fructooligosaccharides

Can increase bifidobacteria and improve stool frequency, but gas/bloating are common. Benefits depend on dose and baseline microbiome. Start with small amounts (for example 2–3 g/day) before increasing.

### Partially hydrolysed guar gum

Often well tolerated and can improve stool regularity/constipation in some people. Evidence is less broad than for psyllium but it is a reasonable GI-directed fibre.

### Resistant starch

Can increase colonic fermentation and short-chain fatty acid production, but clinical benefits are inconsistent and depend on source/dose. Whole-food resistant starch from legumes, oats, barley and cooked/cooled starches is an easy first option.

### Wheat dextrin and generic “fibre blends”

May increase fibre intake but do not necessarily reproduce the LDL-lowering effect of viscous fibres. Product claims should not be generalized across fibre types.

## Probiotics

“Probiotic” is not one treatment. Effects are **strain-, dose-, duration- and indication-specific**.

Potential evidence-backed uses include selected products for:

- prevention of some antibiotic-associated diarrhoea;
- selected irritable-bowel symptoms;
- some infectious diarrhoea settings;
- selected constipation outcomes.

There is no robust evidence that a generic probiotic capsule extends lifespan in healthy adults, permanently “fixes” the microbiome or is necessary for everyone.

### How to evaluate a probiotic

A credible label should state:

1. genus + species + **strain identifier**;
2. colony-forming units through end of shelf life;
3. storage requirements;
4. indication supported by human trials using that strain/product.

A product listing only “10 billion probiotics” without strains is weak evidence.

### Safety

Usually safe in healthy adults, but rare bloodstream infections have occurred in severely immunocompromised or critically ill patients. These populations need clinician guidance.

## Prebiotics and fermented foods

Prebiotics are substrates used by gut microbes. Food sources include onions, garlic, leeks, asparagus, oats, barley, legumes, slightly green bananas and many plant fibres.

Fermented foods such as yogurt/kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, tempeh and miso can add microbial/metabolic diversity. Small randomized trials show interesting immune/microbiome changes, but there is no direct evidence that fermented foods or probiotic supplements extend human lifespan.

## Greens powders / AG1-type products

These products can be convenient, but they should be judged as fortified supplements rather than as replacements for vegetables, fruit, legumes and whole grains.

Problems:

- proprietary blends can hide ingredient doses;
- many included botanical doses are below amounts used in trials;
- fibre is usually far lower than whole produce;
- long ingredient lists increase interaction/contamination uncertainty;
- clinical outcome evidence for the final multi-ingredient product is usually absent.

For a person already eating a high-quality diet, the expected marginal benefit is small.

## Practical hierarchy

1. Whole plant foods + seafood/algal omega-3 where appropriate.
2. Psyllium if fibre intake, LDL or bowel regularity justify it.
3. Targeted algal/fish EPA+DHA if diet is low in seafood.
4. Strain-specific probiotic only for a defined indication.
5. Avoid paying a large premium for generic microbiome/greens powders without product-specific outcome evidence.

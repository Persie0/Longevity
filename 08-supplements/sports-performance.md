# Sports and performance supplements

This file separates supplements that reliably improve a defined performance outcome from products that merely have plausible mechanisms or marketing momentum.

## Quick ranking

### A — strong evidence for the right goal
- creatine monohydrate;
- adequate protein / protein powder when needed;
- caffeine.

### B — useful for specific events/training contexts
- beta-alanine;
- dietary nitrate / beetroot;
- sodium bicarbonate;
- carbohydrate during prolonged endurance;
- electrolytes/sodium when sweat losses are substantial.

### C — plausible or modest/situational evidence
- collagen/gelatin for tendon adaptation;
- citrulline/citrulline malate;
- HMB in selected older/frail/untrained states;
- taurine;
- EAA when complete protein is impractical.

### D — usually not worth it for a well-fed healthy athlete
- BCAA;
- glutamine;
- routine L-carnitine;
- most “recovery blends”;
- nitric-oxide blends without disclosed nitrate/citrulline doses.

## 1. Creatine monohydrate — Grade A

### What it does

Creatine increases intramuscular phosphocreatine availability, supporting rapid ATP resynthesis during repeated high-intensity efforts. Unlike many sports supplements, the practical outcomes are supported by a large trial literature.

Best-supported outcomes:
- strength;
- repeated sprint/high-intensity performance;
- training volume;
- modest additional lean-mass gain when combined with resistance training;
- potentially useful preservation of muscle/function in older adults when combined with training.

### Effect sizes

A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis in adults under 50 found creatine plus resistance training increased lean body mass by roughly **1.14 kg** more than resistance training alone and modestly reduced fat percentage/fat mass.

A separate 2024 strength meta-analysis found creatine plus resistance training increased upper-body strength by about **4.43 kg** and lower-body strength by about **11.35 kg** more than placebo conditions across the pooled measures.

These pooled kilograms are not a promise for an individual lift; studies used different exercises and testing protocols. The consistent direction is more important than the exact number.

Sources:
- PMID 39074168
- PMID 39519498

### Dose

**Simple protocol:**
- 3–5 g creatine monohydrate every day.

**Optional loading:**
- ~20 g/day split into 4 doses for 5–7 days;
- then 3–5 g/day.

Loading only saturates muscle faster. It is not required. Taking 3–5 g/day reaches similar saturation more gradually.

### Timing

Timing is low priority. Daily adherence matters more than pre/post-workout timing.

### Form

**Creatine monohydrate** is the reference form. More expensive forms—HCl, buffered creatine, ethyl ester, “micronized advanced matrix” products—have not demonstrated superiority sufficient to justify replacing monohydrate.

### Cycling

No cycling is required.

### Body weight and water

A small early increase in body weight can occur because muscle creatine increases intracellular water. This is not the same as fat gain.

### Kidney safety and creatinine

A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found a small increase in serum creatinine but no significant reduction in pooled GFR. Creatinine is partly a creatine breakdown product, so a creatinine-based eGFR can look worse without true kidney damage.

Source: PMID 41199218, DOI 10.1186/s12882-025-04558-6.

Practical caveat: known kidney disease, unexplained renal abnormalities or nephrotoxic medication use should be discussed with a clinician. If renal function is being monitored, tell the clinician you take creatine; cystatin-C or measured GFR can sometimes be more informative than creatinine alone.

## 2. Protein powders — Grade A/B

Protein powder is **food convenience**, not a special anabolic drug.

### When it helps

It is useful when:
- reaching ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day from food is inconvenient;
- appetite is low;
- a quick post-training meal is useful;
- plant-based diets need an easy high-quality protein source;
- older adults struggle to get enough protein per meal.

If total protein is already sufficient, adding extra shakes generally gives diminishing returns.

### Types

#### Whey
- rapidly digested;
- leucine-rich;
- very practical around training;
- concentrate is cheaper; isolate has less lactose/fat.

#### Casein / milk protein
- slower digestion;
- useful as any normal protein source, including before a long overnight fast if desired;
- no need to force bedtime casein if daily protein is already good.

#### Soy isolate
- excellent plant option;
- strong amino-acid profile;
- suitable for muscle gain when total intake is adequate.

#### Pea / rice blends
- complementary amino-acid profiles;
- good vegan option.

### Dose

Typical serving: **20–40 g protein**, adjusted to body size and meal composition.

For hypertrophy, see `../03-muscle/protein-and-hypertrophy.md`.

### BCAA vs complete protein

A complete protein supplies all essential amino acids. BCAA alone does not. If the choice is whey/soy/pea blend versus BCAA, choose the complete protein.

## 3. Caffeine — Grade A for acute performance

### Outcomes

ISSN’s position stand concludes caffeine can improve:
- aerobic endurance;
- muscular endurance;
- movement velocity;
- some strength/power tasks;
- sprint/jump/throw performance;
- vigilance/alertness.

Endurance has the most consistent benefit.

### Dose

Evidence is strongest around **3–6 mg/kg**. Lower doses near **1–3 mg/kg** often work and are a sensible practical starting point.

Very high doses around **9 mg/kg** add side effects and are not required for performance.

Example at 75 kg:
- 1 mg/kg = 75 mg;
- 2 mg/kg = 150 mg;
- 3 mg/kg = 225 mg;
- 6 mg/kg = 450 mg.

### Timing

Commonly ~30–60 min before exercise. Gum can act faster.

### Expected magnitude

Endurance time-trial meta-analytic estimates often land in the low-single-digit percentage range; one synthesis cited by ISSN reported average time-trial improvement around **2.3%**, with substantial individual variability.

### The main downside: sleep

A supplement that adds 1–2% to one workout but damages sleep repeatedly can be a bad trade for long-term training and health.

Caffeine half-life varies widely. A practical rule is to avoid it for roughly **8–10 hours before intended sleep** if sleep is sensitive, then adjust based on your response.

### Other cautions

High dose can cause:
- anxiety/jitters;
- tachycardia/palpitations;
- GI symptoms;
- headache;
- increased blood pressure;
- tremor.

Pregnancy, arrhythmias, uncontrolled hypertension, panic/anxiety disorders and stimulant medications need extra caution.

Source: ISSN position stand, DOI 10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4.

## 4. Beta-alanine — Grade B

### What it targets

Beta-alanine raises muscle carnosine, improving intracellular buffering during hard efforts where acidosis contributes to fatigue.

### Best use case

The evidence is strongest for very hard exercise lasting roughly **0.5–10 minutes**, repeated intervals and exercise-capacity tests.

A 2017 meta-analysis of 40 studies/1,461 participants found a small overall effect (**ES 0.18**), with larger effects for exercise capacity in the relevant duration window.

Source: PMID 27797728, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2016-096396.

### Dose

Common loading range:
- **4–6.4 g/day**;
- split into smaller doses;
- for at least **4 weeks**.

It is a chronic loading supplement. Taking one scoop 20 minutes before a workout because a pre-workout contains beta-alanine is not how the mechanism works.

### Side effects

Paresthesia (tingling/itching), especially with large single doses. Divided doses or sustained-release formulations reduce this.

### Who does not need it

A recreational lifter doing ordinary sets with long rests may notice little. It matters more for rowers, track cycling, combat sports, CrossFit-style efforts, repeated sprints and other high-intensity work in the target duration.

## 5. Dietary nitrate / beetroot — Grade B

### What it does

Dietary nitrate is converted through the nitrate→nitrite→nitric-oxide pathway and can reduce oxygen cost and influence vascular/muscle function.

### Effect size

A 2022 meta-analysis of **123 studies and 1,705 participants** found a small overall exercise-performance effect (**SMD 0.101**). Beetroot juice and high-nitrate diets appeared more consistently effective than nitrate salts.

Source: PMID 35580578, DOI 10.1093/advances/nmac054.

### Dose/timing

A common evidence-based range is:
- roughly **5–9 mmol nitrate** (~300–600 mg nitrate);
- around **2–3 hours pre-exercise**.

Some protocols use several days of dosing before competition.

### Oral bacteria matter

Antibacterial mouthwash can reduce conversion of nitrate to nitrite. Avoid strong antibacterial mouthwash around nitrate dosing when using it for performance.

### Product variability

“Beetroot powder” is not automatically an effective nitrate product. Unless nitrate content is standardized/disclosed, the actual ergogenic dose can be unknown.

## 6. Sodium bicarbonate — Grade B

### Best use

High-intensity exercise where extracellular buffering helps:
- repeated sprints;
- 1–10-minute hard efforts;
- combat/rowing/swimming/cycling events;
- some high-volume resistance circuits.

### Dose

ISSN position stand:
- minimum effective single dose around **0.2 g/kg**;
- optimal common dose around **0.3 g/kg**;
- usually **60–180 min before exercise**.

At 75 kg, 0.3 g/kg = **22.5 g sodium bicarbonate**, which is a large amount.

### Side effects

Main problem: GI distress—bloating, nausea, abdominal pain, diarrhea/vomiting. Individual timing, food and split/capsule strategies matter.

Higher single doses (0.4–0.5 g/kg) usually add side effects rather than benefit.

Source: PMID 34503527, DOI 10.1186/s12970-021-00458-w.

### Sodium caution

The acute sodium load is large. People with hypertension, heart/kidney disease or sodium-sensitive conditions should not treat this as a casual supplement.

## 7. Carbohydrate powders, gels and sports drinks — Grade B for endurance

Carbohydrate supplements are valuable **fuel**, not magic.

### When useful

- prolonged endurance sessions;
- long races;
- repeated training sessions with limited recovery;
- very high weekly endurance volume;
- situations where solid food is impractical.

### General dosing framework

Common contemporary endurance guidance:
- shorter exercise: often no carbohydrate needed;
- ~1–2.5 h: commonly ~30–60 g/hour;
- very prolonged hard endurance: up to ~90 g/hour using multiple transportable carbohydrates, with gut training.

Some elite protocols go higher, but 90+ g/h is not a beginner default.

### Gym sessions

A normal 45–75-minute resistance session rarely requires an intra-workout sugar drink if normal meals provide adequate carbohydrate.

## 8. Electrolytes — Grade B when losses justify them

### Useful when

- long-duration exercise;
- hot/humid conditions;
- high sweat rate;
- salty sweaters;
- repeated sessions with limited recovery.

### Not a universal daily supplement

For a sedentary person eating a normal diet, electrolyte powders often just add sodium, flavor and cost.

### Personalization

Fluid/sodium needs depend on:
- body size;
- sweat rate;
- sweat sodium concentration;
- weather;
- exercise duration;
- acclimatization.

Avoid overdrinking plain water in very long events; exercise-associated hyponatremia is a real risk.

## 9. Collagen / gelatin — Grade C/B for tendon, not muscle protein

### Evidence

A 2026 systematic review included **8 RCTs, 257 participants**. Most trials combined collagen with resistance/plyometric loading. Higher-dose trials around **15–30 g/day**, typically with vitamin C, more often showed improvements in tendon cross-sectional area or stiffness. The review found no additive muscle-strength effect.

Source: PMID 41900537.

### Practical interpretation

Potentially reasonable for:
- tendon rehabilitation/loading programs;
- athletes with very high tendon demands;
- people who want to experiment after basic rehab/loading/nutrition are addressed.

A common protocol in studies/practice:
- ~15 g collagen/gelatin;
- ≥50 mg vitamin C;
- around a tendon-loading session.

But evidence is still based on small heterogeneous trials.

### Important limitation

Collagen has a poor essential-amino-acid profile for skeletal muscle hypertrophy. Do not replace whey/soy/food protein with collagen to hit the main daily muscle-protein target.

## 10. Essential amino acids (EAA) — Grade B/D depending context

EAAs can stimulate muscle protein synthesis because they provide all amino acids that cannot be synthesized.

Reasonable when:
- a complete meal is unavailable;
- calorie volume must be extremely low;
- clinical/older-adult feeding situations make full protein difficult.

For most healthy lifters, a 25–40 g serving of complete protein is cheaper/simpler and provides the same practical function.

## 11. BCAA — Grade D for hypertrophy when protein is adequate

BCAA products contain leucine, isoleucine and valine but omit the other essential amino acids needed to sustain new protein synthesis.

Some systematic reviews/meta-analyses show reductions in:
- delayed-onset muscle soreness;
- creatine kinase after damaging exercise.

But recovery of actual muscle performance is much less consistent, and no robust evidence shows additional hypertrophy when total high-quality protein is already adequate.

Practical verdict: **buy complete protein instead**.

## 12. Glutamine — Grade D for healthy athletes

Glutamine is important physiologically and medically, but that does not make routine supplementation ergogenic.

A systematic review/meta-analysis of **47 studies** found no general benefit for aerobic performance, body composition or athlete immune outcomes.

Source: PMID 29784526, DOI 10.1016/j.clnu.2018.05.001.

Use in specialized clinical nutrition is a different question.

## 13. Citrulline / citrulline malate — Grade C/B

Citrulline can raise arginine and nitric-oxide availability. Acute studies/meta-analyses often report small improvements in repetitions to failure, perceived effort or blood flow, but results are less consistent than caffeine/creatine.

Common study dosing:
- ~6–8 g citrulline malate before training;
- or several grams of pure L-citrulline.

Major problem: “citrulline malate 2:1” labeling does not always reflect a standardized chemical mixture, so products can differ substantially.

Reasonable optional experiment after creatine/protein/sleep/training are optimized; not a core longevity or hypertrophy supplement.

## 14. HMB — Grade C/B

HMB is a leucine metabolite. Evidence is most plausible in:
- older/frail adults;
- immobilization/inactivity;
- very low training status;
- catabolic settings.

Effects in well-trained young lifters are inconsistent and generally small. Adequate protein and progressive resistance training are much more important.

Common study dose: ~**3 g/day**.

## 15. Taurine — Grade C

Taurine has interesting roles in osmoregulation, bile acids, calcium handling and cell protection. Human trials/meta-analyses suggest possible small cardiometabolic and performance effects in some contexts, while animal aging research has generated substantial publicity.

What is **not** established:
- that taurine supplementation extends human lifespan;
- a universal anti-aging dose;
- a meaningful advantage over the core diet/training interventions.

Treat it as promising research, not a required stack component.

## 16. L-carnitine — Grade D/C

Carnitine is essential to fatty-acid transport into mitochondria. Oral supplementation in already sufficient healthy people does not reliably translate into improved endurance/fat loss.

Potential benefits are more relevant to:
- true deficiency;
- selected clinical populations;
- possibly recovery endpoints in specific studies.

Routine “fat burner” marketing is not supported.

## 17. Pre-workout blends

### What usually works inside them

Often the effective ingredients are simply:
- caffeine;
- creatine (if taken daily, although pre-workout timing is irrelevant);
- beta-alanine (if enough is taken chronically);
- nitrate/citrulline when standardized and adequately dosed.

### Why single ingredients are better

- known dose;
- easier to identify side effects;
- cheaper;
- less duplicate stimulant exposure;
- lower uncertainty from proprietary blends.

Do not confuse tingling from beta-alanine with proof that a pre-workout is “working.”

## 18. “Pump” products

A larger acute pump is not equivalent to more hypertrophy. Citrulline/nitrate can affect blood flow, but the central drivers of hypertrophy remain:
- progressive resistance training;
- sufficient hard-set volume;
- adequate protein/energy;
- recovery.

## 19. What to prioritize by athlete type

### Recreational gym-goer
1. Protein adequacy.
2. Creatine 3–5 g/day.
3. Caffeine only if desired and sleep-safe.
4. Nothing else required.

### Hypertrophy-focused lifter
1. Protein ~1.6 g/kg/day.
2. Creatine.
3. Carbohydrate adequate for training.
4. Optional caffeine.
5. Citrulline only as optional marginal gain.
6. BCAA/glutamine not needed.

### Middle-distance/repeated high-intensity athlete
1. Carbohydrate availability.
2. Caffeine.
3. Beta-alanine.
4. Sodium bicarbonate if GI tolerance is solved.
5. Nitrate/beetroot.
6. Creatine depending event/training demands.

### Long endurance athlete
1. Carbohydrate fueling.
2. Fluids/electrolytes tailored to losses.
3. Caffeine.
4. Nitrate/beetroot optional.
5. Protein/creatine for strength training and recovery as relevant.

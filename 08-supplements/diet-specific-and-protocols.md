# Diet-specific supplement needs and practical protocols

## Principle

Supplements should solve a specific problem. The need changes with dietary pattern, age, training type, sun exposure, laboratory status and medical conditions.

A useful sequence is:

1. assess diet and lifestyle;
2. identify realistic nutrient/performance gaps;
3. use food first where practical;
4. test when a laboratory value meaningfully changes management;
5. supplement the gap at an evidence-based dose;
6. reassess instead of stacking indefinitely.

## Healthy omnivorous resistance-training adult

### Minimal evidence-based default

**Daily / regular**

- Creatine monohydrate: **3–5 g/day**.
- Protein: reach about **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day**, with ~1.6 g/kg/day a strong target for modest hypertrophy. Use whey/casein/soy/pea blend only if food intake falls short.
- Fibre: aim **~30–40 g/day from food**; add psyllium **5–10 g/day** if intake is low or LDL/constipation provides another reason.

**Conditional**

- Vitamin D: based on low intake/sun exposure, risk factors and/or measured 25-OH-D; avoid routine megadoses.
- EPA/DHA: food first; consider **~500–1,000 mg/day EPA+DHA** if fish intake is consistently low.
- Caffeine: **~1–3 mg/kg** 30–60 min pre-training when desired, lower than classic 3–6 mg/kg if sensitivity/sleep matters.
- Electrolyte/carbohydrate drinks: only for long/hot/high-sweat endurance sessions or very high training volume.

### Usually unnecessary

BCAAs, glutamine, HMB, testosterone boosters, greens powders, routine multivitamins, mega-antioxidants, NMN/NR, resveratrol and elaborate pre-workout stacks.

## Vegan / plant-based athlete

### High-priority nutrients

#### Vitamin B12 — essential

Fortified foods or supplementation is required because unfortified plant foods are not reliable sources.

Common practical regimens include:

- **50–100 micrograms/day**, or
- **~1,000 micrograms 2–3 times/week**,

with exact strategy depending on product, adherence and laboratory status.

Testing can include B12 plus methylmalonic acid when status is uncertain.

#### Protein

Target roughly **1.4–1.8 g/kg/day** for a resistance-trained vegan, with the upper end useful when protein quality/digestibility is lower.

Best anchors:

- tofu/tempeh/edamame;
- soy milk/yogurt;
- lentils/beans/chickpeas;
- seitan when gluten is tolerated;
- soy isolate or pea+rice blend.

A serving providing **~25–40 g protein** per main meal works well.

#### Iodine

Use iodized salt consistently unless another reliable source exists. Seaweed iodine is highly variable; kelp can easily overshoot safe intake.

A supplement around the adult RDA (**150 micrograms/day**) can be reasonable when iodized salt and seafood/dairy are absent. Thyroid disease changes the decision.

#### Omega-3

Eat ALA-rich foods daily: ground flax, chia, walnuts, canola. Consider algae-derived **250–500 mg/day DHA+EPA** for a direct long-chain source.

#### Calcium

Use calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milk/yogurt and low-oxalate greens. Supplement only the dietary shortfall rather than automatically taking 1,000 mg as a pill.

#### Vitamin D

Same principle as omnivores: supplement when sun exposure/intake/status justifies it.

#### Iron

Plant iron is less bioavailable. Combine legumes/whole grains/seeds with vitamin-C-rich foods. Do **not** take iron routinely without evidence of low stores or a clinician-defined need.

Ferritin, haemoglobin and transferrin saturation can guide decisions.

#### Zinc and selenium

Whole grains, legumes, nuts/seeds provide zinc, but phytate lowers absorption. Soaking/sprouting/fermentation helps. Brazil nuts contain selenium but concentrations vary dramatically; they should not be used as an uncontrolled megadose.

## Vegetarian but includes dairy/eggs

B12 risk is lower than in vegan diets but still depends on actual intake. Iron, iodine, omega-3 and protein quality deserve attention. Dairy can make calcium and iodine much easier to obtain.

## Low-fish diet

If fish/seafood is absent:

- prioritize ALA-rich plants;
- consider algae EPA/DHA, particularly in pregnancy or strictly plant-based diets;
- do not assume fish-oil capsules replicate all benefits of eating fish.

## Low-dairy diet

Key issue is usually calcium, not a special need for “dairy replacement supplements.” Use:

- calcium-set tofu;
- fortified soy/other plant milks;
- yogurt alternatives with calcium;
- canned sardines/salmon with bones if omnivorous;
- low-oxalate greens.

Supplement enough calcium to close the gap rather than taking large boluses. Split doses of **≤500 mg elemental calcium** are generally better absorbed.

## Older adult focused on muscle and bone preservation

### High-value baseline

- Resistance training 2–3+ days/week.
- Protein **~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** if healthy and active, distributed across 3–4 meals.
- Each meal often **~0.3–0.4 g/kg protein**, sometimes more because anabolic resistance increases with age.
- Creatine monohydrate **3–5 g/day** can support strength/lean mass when paired with training.
- Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D status.

### Conditional additions

- Protein powder if appetite or chewing/cooking burden makes food targets hard.
- Vitamin B12 if absorption/intake is poor, especially with metformin, proton-pump inhibitors, gastric surgery or restrictive diets.
- Omega-3 if fish intake is low; evidence for direct anti-sarcopenia effects is less consistent than for protein + resistance training.

### Fall/fracture prevention priorities

Supplements are secondary to:

- progressive strength training;
- balance training;
- vision/medication review;
- adequate energy/protein;
- osteoporosis diagnosis/treatment when indicated.

## Endurance athlete

### Performance priorities

1. **Carbohydrate availability** is often more important than exotic supplements.
2. For sessions/races >60–90 min, use carbohydrate during exercise. Typical evidence-based range: **30–60 g/hour**, and up to **~90 g/hour** for long events using multiple transportable carbohydrates after gut training.
3. Sodium/fluid should reflect sweat rate, climate and event duration; do not force fluid beyond thirst to the point of hyponatraemia risk.
4. Caffeine **1–3 mg/kg** can improve performance with fewer side effects than very high doses.
5. Nitrate/beetroot can help some endurance/time-trial outcomes, especially in less highly trained athletes. Typical nitrate dose **~5–9 mmol (~300–600 mg nitrate)** 2–3 h before exercise or over several days.
6. Beta-alanine is most useful for repeated/high-intensity efforts lasting roughly 1–4 min, not easy endurance volume.
7. Sodium bicarbonate **~0.2–0.3 g/kg** can improve high-intensity performance but GI effects are common; test in training, never first on race day.

### Iron

Endurance athletes—especially menstruating athletes, distance runners and those restricting calories—have higher iron-deficiency risk. Ferritin/haemoglobin testing is far better than blind supplementation.

## Cutting / calorie restriction

During a fat-loss phase:

- maintain resistance training;
- protein often **~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** depending on leanness/deficit/training;
- preserve fruit, vegetables, legumes and micronutrient-dense foods;
- a multivitamin may be reasonable in unusually restrictive diets, but it does not replace food and should avoid megadoses.

Aggressive chronic energy restriction can impair bone, hormones, immune function and training performance.

## Example minimalist protocols

### Protocol A — healthy omnivore gym-goer

- Creatine monohydrate: 3–5 g/day.
- Protein powder: 20–40 g serving only when needed to reach target.
- Psyllium: 5 g once/twice daily if dietary fibre/LDL/constipation warrants.
- Caffeine: optional 1–3 mg/kg pre-workout, not within ~8–10 h of bedtime if sleep is affected.
- Everything else: targeted only.

### Protocol B — vegan lifter

- B12: reliable supplement/fortified-food strategy.
- Creatine: 3–5 g/day.
- Protein powder: soy or pea+rice as needed.
- Iodine: iodized salt or ~150 micrograms/day if intake is otherwise unreliable.
- Algal DHA/EPA: 250–500 mg/day optional/reasonable.
- Vitamin D/calcium/iron/zinc: only when diet/status indicates.

### Protocol C — older adult

- Protein to 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day.
- Creatine 3–5 g/day if kidney function/medical context permits.
- Vitamin D/calcium based on status/intake/osteoporosis plan.
- B12 when intake/absorption is inadequate.
- Resistance + balance training remains more important than any pill.

### Protocol D — endurance athlete

- Carbohydrate around/during long sessions.
- Sodium/fluids individualized to sweat/environment.
- Caffeine 1–3 mg/kg for key events.
- Nitrate/beetroot when useful and tolerated.
- Beta-alanine/bicarbonate only when event demands match the mechanism.
- Iron only after testing/clinical assessment.

## What NOT to buy by default

- proprietary “longevity stacks”;
- BCAA powders when protein is already adequate;
- mega-dose antioxidant stacks;
- testosterone boosters;
- expensive greens powders as a vegetable replacement;
- detox/cleanse powders;
- fat burners;
- stimulant-heavy proprietary pre-workouts;
- megadose vitamin D, zinc, selenium or iodine without a reason;
- NMN/NR/resveratrol solely because they alter a biomarker;
- unverified bodybuilding/sexual-enhancement products.
